# Same paper, three journals (contrast)

## Tertius Dolens : Interalter Conflict and Its Negative Impact on Broker Performance (Organization Science, 2026)

**Organization Science**: fit 55/100. We value the fresh theoretical move from a "benefiting third" (tertius gaudens) to a "suffering third" (tertius dolens), which questions a core assumption of network theory, but the abstract does not yet say what setting, data, or identification strategy will show that conflict between alters causes the innovation penalty rather than simply going along with weaker brokers.
- *[methods]* **Pin down the empirical design. Use a defined interfirm network, such as R&D alliances in biopharma or semiconductors, and measure conflict between alters with observable events like patent litigation between partners. Use the timing of lawsuits that start for unrelated reasons as a source of quasi-exogenous variation.**: Reviewers here expect credible evidence that interalter conflict causes the drop in innovation, and a litigation-onset design separates that conflict from partner selection and from the broker's own prior quality.
- *[mechanism]* **Test the three proposed mechanisms separately: constrained knowledge flows (patent citations to each alter), coordination costs (alliance terminations and project delays), and reputational damage (fewer new partners after the conflict begins).**: This journal rewards contributions that open up the black box of how network positions work, and separating the mechanisms turns a label into an explanatory theory.
- *[mechanism]* **Add a qualitative or process component showing how brokers actively manage their position. Do they take sides, compartmentalize the two relationships, or exit one of them? Do these responses change how the mitigating conditions work?**: Organization Science values agency and the dynamics of organizing, and theorizing broker responses would link structural-hole theory to relational work and to research on tertius iungens (the broker who joins others together).

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 40/100. SMJ would value a theoretically sharp move that turns brokerage from an advantage into a liability for firm innovation, but it would question how interalter conflict is measured and identified, because the abstract gives no empirical setting, data, or design, and it would want the conflict channel separated from selection into broker positions.
- *[methods]* **Ground the theory in a specific setting, such as alliance networks in which partner conflict is observable through patent litigation, alliance termination, or public disputes, and use plausibly exogenous conflict onset (for example, lawsuits between alters triggered by court rulings) in a difference-in-differences design around the broker's innovation.**: SMJ increasingly expects causal leverage on network effects, and if conflict is endogenous to the broker's own position the main claim cannot be believed.
- *[mechanism]* **Test the three proposed mechanisms (restricted knowledge exchange, coordination costs, reputational damage) separately, using knowledge-flow measures such as citations to alters, alliance formation lags, and new partner acquisition.**: Sorting out the mechanisms turns a renamed construct into a contribution to strategy theory about when network resources become liabilities.
- *[practice]* **Frame the broker's response as a strategic choice: whether to take sides, exit one tie, or stay balanced, and how those choices shape innovation and later network position.**: SMJ rewards implications for firm strategy and managerial action, not only structural contingencies.

**Management Science**: fit 22/100. Management Science would value the new idea that conflict between a firm's partners can turn brokerage into a liability, but would question whether the mechanism is causally identified and precisely specified, because the abstract as framed reads as a construct-extension paper in the style of organization theory.
- *[methods]* **Identify interalter conflict with exogenous shocks, for example patent litigation filed between two alliance partners or partner disputes caused by mergers, and estimate effects on the broker's innovation in a staggered difference-in-differences design with matched brokers whose partners are not in conflict.**: This journal expects causal evidence, and conflict between partners is likely endogenous to the broker's own strategy and performance.
- *[mechanism]* **Separate the three proposed mechanisms (blocked knowledge flow, coordination cost, reputation damage) using measurable outcomes: citation flows through each alter, alliance terminations and delays, and how often third parties form new ties with the broker.**: Reviewers here reward work that pins down a mechanism and measures its size, rather than offering a bundle of accounts that all predict the same thing.
- *[theory]* **Build a simple formal model of the broker's choice to keep, drop, or rebalance ties when its partners are in conflict. Derive predictions about when status, multiplexity, or ties that span conflict-free structural holes offset the costs, and draw out what this means for managing an alliance portfolio.**: Pairing a formal model with empirical tests, and giving managers an actionable decision rule, matches what this journal publishes in strategy and networks.

## Shaping Ecosystem Rules: Complementarities, Interdependencies, and Firms’ Success in Coordinating Ecosystems Via Standard-Setting (Organization Science, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 62/100. We value the attempt to combine relational and technological-interdependence views of ecosystem governance into a socio-technical trade-off, but we would ask whether this is really a theory of organizing (how authority, influence and consensus come about) or just a strategy result about who wins standards.
- *[theory]* **Present the trade-off as a theory of how influence works under distributed authority. Draw explicitly on theories of political coalitions, consensus decision making and interorganizational coordination, so that adjustment costs are treated as a source of partner defection.**: Organization Science rewards contributions to organization theory, such as governance, power and coordination, more than contributions that only predict which firms win.
- *[mechanism]* **Test the proposed mechanism directly. Measure which tied partners face high adjustment costs, and check whether those partners withhold support, abstain or oppose in votes and comment records.**: Showing the partner-level mechanism, and not only the outcome correlation, would make the relational-cost argument convincing to reviewers.
- *[mechanism]* **Add qualitative evidence from meeting minutes, mailing lists or interviews. Use it to show how firms manage the trade-off, for example by breaking up core proposals, sequencing them, or compensating partners.**: A mixed-methods account of the micro-processes of shaping fits the journal's appetite for process insight, and it moves the paper from a moderation finding toward actionable theory.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 72/100. SMJ would value how the paper brings relational and technological-interdependence views of ecosystems together into one strategic trade-off over who shapes the rules. Reviewers would question whether "shaping success" is identified separately from proposal quality and from the fact that central firms choose which proposals to make, and whether the trade-off has consequences for performance or competitive advantage.
- *[methods]* **Model selection into core proposals and into relational position. Use within-firm variation across proposals, and if possible use exogenous shocks to ties (such as M&A among members or member exits) or to how central a proposal is.**: SMJ reviewers increasingly expect credible identification of the mechanism, not just associations from standards data.
- *[mechanism]* **Measure partners' adjustment costs directly, for example through their patent or product overlap with the components affected. Show that opposition or abstention comes specifically from tied partners who are exposed to those costs.**: Showing the mechanism of the socio-technical trade-off at the level of individual partners would make the theoretical contribution much stronger than inferring it from an interaction term.
- *[practice]* **Link shaping success to downstream value capture, such as essential-patent licensing, market share in standard-compliant products, or stock market reactions. Also analyze how firms manage the trade-off strategically, for example by splitting core proposals into smaller pieces or by co-sponsoring them.**: SMJ prizes implications for value creation and capture and for firm strategy, and this would turn a governance finding into a contribution about competitive advantage.

**Management Science**: fit 38/100. Management Science would value a sharp, generalizable mechanism behind ecosystem rule-shaping, but it would question whether the socio-technical trade-off is causally identified and quantified, rather than just framed in the language of strategy and organization theory.
- *[theory]* **Formalize the trade-off in a simple model where a proposer's ties confer influence while the proposal's coreness raises partners' adjustment costs, and derive testable comparative statics, such as when ties stop helping.**: The journal rewards tight theory with sharp, non-obvious predictions that can be falsified, not just integrated perspectives.
- *[methods]* **Identify the effect with plausibly exogenous variation, such as partner mergers or exits that shift relational ties, or architectural changes that shift a proposal's coreness, using proposal-level data from bodies like 3GPP or IETF.**: Reviewers here expect causal evidence and economically meaningful magnitudes rather than correlations from observational panels.
- *[mechanism]* **Measure the adjustment-cost mechanism directly, for example by testing whether opposition comes specifically from partners whose components depend on the proposal, and link shaping success to downstream outcomes such as essential patents or market share.**: Pinning down the mechanism and its consequences for performance turns a descriptive finding into managerial and policy implications for standards and platform governance.

## Firm growth and stagnation in the United States: Key trends and new data opportunities (Strategic Management Journal, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 8/100. We would value the chance to see the whole distribution of firm growth, including stagnant and shrinking firms, at population scale, but as written this is a data resource aimed at strategic management, not a theory-building contribution about organizations, and we would ask what organizational mechanism the dataset helps explain.
- *[theory]* **Build the paper around one theoretical puzzle, such as why stagnant firms persist and fewer young firms reach high growth. Draw on organizational ecology, inertia, or a behavioral theory of the firm to explain this, and test predictions derived from that theory with the BDS-HG distributions.**: Organization Science publishes papers that advance organization theory; a dataset alone is not enough.
- *[data]* **Link the BDS-HG cells to organizational-level data in the restricted Census data, such as LBD establishment structure, multi-unit status, or ownership changes. Use this to test whether organizational form and structure explain how firms move between high-growth, stagnant, and shrinking states.**: Mechanisms at the level of organizations are what this journal's readers expect, rather than descriptions of aggregate trends.
- *[theory]* **Treat stagnation as a distinct organizational state, not just a residual category. Theorize how firms enter and leave it, and how this depends on firm age and on the competitive environment.**: Calling stagnation a phenomenon that existing growth and learning theories have overlooked gives the paper a conceptual contribution that fits the journal.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 45/100. SMJ values data resources that help strategy research build cumulative knowledge, as its replication and methods pieces show, but the board will ask what this dataset teaches us about firm strategy and competitive advantage, beyond a descriptive account of US business dynamics.
- *[positioning]* **Build the paper around a strategy question, such as whether the fall in high-growth firms and the rise in stagnant firms reflect more persistent incumbent advantage, more industry concentration, or weaker entry, and test this using industry-by-age-by-size variation.**: SMJ publishes work that advances theory on why firm performance differs, so the descriptive trends need to be linked to resource-based or competitive-dynamics explanations.
- *[methods]* **Turn the benchmarking idea into a method: show how common strategy samples (e.g., Compustat, VC-backed startups, Crunchbase) compare with the full BDS-HG growth distribution, and show how reweighting changes published estimates.**: Representativeness and generalizability are central concerns in SMJ's replication and methods conversations, so a demonstrated correction tool would make the contribution concrete.
- *[positioning]* **Replace the three loosely connected applications with one or two deep applications tied to active SMJ debates, such as entrepreneurial strategy and scaling, or the persistence of firm performance.**: Focused applications show the dataset's theoretical value better than a broad tour of uses.

**Management Science**: fit 14/100. We value the Census data and how much of the growth distribution it covers, but this journal publishes papers that identify a mechanism behind firm growth or test a theory about it, and a paper that mainly introduces a dataset and describes its trends, written for strategy researchers, doesn't count as a contribution here.
- *[positioning]* **Shift the paper's main question from introducing the dataset to explaining one result: why high-growth firms became less common and stagnant firms more common. Test competing explanations against each other, for example incumbent concentration, intangible capital, and limits on how easily resources move between firms, using variation across industries, regions, and cohorts.**: Papers in Management Science need an answer to a causal or theoretical question; documenting trends alone is not enough.
- *[methods]* **Use a quasi-experimental shock, such as state noncompete reforms, changes in bank deregulation, or China import competition, to estimate how policy or market frictions shift where firms fall across the high-growth, stagnant and shrinking categories.**: The journal rewards credible identification, and the strength of BDS-HG is that it covers the whole growth distribution, so it can show effects that a sample of only high-growth firms would miss.
- *[theory]* **Build a short formal model or a set of decomposition results linking how firms select into growth categories, how they learn, and how resources are reallocated, and use the BDS-HG patterns to test the model's predictions.**: Pairing a model with large-scale evidence matches the kind of contribution this journal values and makes the paper about more than the data.

## Absolute, average‐based, and rank‐based aspirations (Strategic Management Journal, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 55/100. Organization Science would value a formal model that overturns an established result on optimal aspirations by showing that social versus absolute aspiration specification matters. It would ask whether the "simple" model reaches beyond individual bandit search to say something about organizing: how aspirations are set socially, who the comparison others are, and what happens when everyone in the population searches against everyone else.
- *[theory]* **Make social aspirations endogenous at the population level, so that peers' search changes the reference point. Model how strongly agents are coupled through rank or average targets, and how that affects the population's diversity in exploration.**: Organization Science values results that come from interaction among actors, such as ecologies of agents and competitive selection, more than results about isolated decision makers.
- *[practice]* **Spell out the organizational design levers. Compare who sets the targets (managers versus self-set), who the reference group is (inside versus outside the unit), and how many slots count as a 'top rank' in a tournament. Turn these into propositions about target setting and incentive design.**: Linking the model to concrete organizational practices would widen its audience beyond formal modelers and strengthen the 'implications for organizations' claim.
- *[data]* **Add an empirical illustration or a calibration test, for example sales teams or R&D units that use relative performance targets in turbulent markets, or an experiment that varies the aspiration format.**: Organization Science often rewards models that generate testable predictions and show at least a plausibility check against real organizational data.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 55/100. SMJ would value a formal, tractable result that overturns a received finding on aspirations and turbulence, which fits its tradition of formal and computational behavioral strategy (e.g., the benchmarking and behavioral real options models). Reviewers would question whether "individuals' exploration" is linked clearly enough to firm-level strategy, competitive advantage, and managerial target-setting choices.
- *[scope]* **Recast the model at the firm or business-unit level, with rivals as the social reference group, so that rank-based aspirations become a matter of competitive positioning and performance heterogeneity across firms.**: SMJ rewards models that speak to why firms differ in performance, and relative aspirations among competing firms connect the result directly to competitive dynamics.
- *[practice]* **Treat the choice of aspiration specification (absolute, average-based, rank-based) as a design lever for managers, and derive conditions under which each one maximizes long-run performance, including mixed or hybrid targets.**: This turns an interesting analytical result into practical guidance on target setting and incentive design, which strategy readers would expect.
- *[theory]* **Derive testable propositions and outline how they could be checked, for example with tournament or ranking settings (sales contests, league tables, analyst rankings) where turbulence varies.**: Pairing formal results with a clear empirical route makes the contribution cumulative and easier for SMJ's largely empirical audience to take up.

**Management Science**: fit 40/100. We value a tractable analytical model that overturns an established comparative static, here the claim that optimal aspirations fall as turbulence rises. We would ask whether the result holds once agents interact strategically and the aspiration is set by a principal, and whether it matters for real target-setting decisions beyond simulation-style behavioral theory.
- *[theory]* **Endogenize aspiration-setting as a design problem. A principal (manager) chooses the target specification (absolute, rank-based or average-based) and its level, and agents respond. Then characterize which specification is optimal as turbulence and population size vary.**: Management Science rewards prescriptive mechanism-design insights, such as optimal contracts, feedback and contest design, more than descriptive behavioral comparative statics.
- *[theory]* **Model the strategic interdependence created by relative aspirations. One agent's exploration shifts others' reference points, so the model should compare equilibrium with socially optimal exploration and connect the analysis to the tournament and contest literature.**: Relative targets create externalities across agents. Deriving their welfare and efficiency implications would link the paper to core Management Science work on contests and incentives.
- *[methods]* **Add an empirical test of the key prediction, for example using sales-force or online-contest data where targets are rank-based or absolute and turbulence varies exogenously.**: Management Science increasingly expects formal predictions to be paired with credible causal evidence, and recent papers on goal-ratcheting field experiments show this kind of pairing.

## Play it Again, Sam? Reference-Point Formation and Product Differentiation in the Music Industry (Management Science, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 42/100. Organization Science would value how the paper adds to work on categories and optimal distinctiveness by setting commercial audiences against legitimating audiences, but it would ask what the paper adds to organization theory beyond a descriptive account of taste in the music industry, and what mechanisms explain how audiences evaluate.
- *[theory]* **Present the paper as a theory of how audiences with different logics (market versus cultural legitimacy) judge a producer's identity at several reference levels: the producer's own past, its peers and the market exemplar. Develop explicit mechanisms such as risk aversion under commercial pressure and critics' need to stand apart from other critics.**: Organization Science publishes work that builds generalizable theory about categories, audiences and identity, not findings that apply only to one industry.
- *[theory]* **Examine the gap between objective and subjective identity, meaning cases where an album's sound diverges from the genres it claims. Test whether this mismatch is penalized or rewarded differently by each audience.**: Separating material features from category claims is the most original contribution to the categories literature, and right now it is underused.
- *[mechanism]* **Model how the two audiences interact over time. For example, test whether critics' acclaim for a novel album later shifts radio's response to the artist's next release, and whether the artist's status moderates these preferences.**: Dynamic interplay between audiences and status contingencies would tie the paper to the journal's interest in legitimation processes and in how producers repositioning their offerings manage conflicting audience demands.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 22/100. SMJ would value the multi-audience take on optimal distinctiveness and how it separates objective from categorical identity. It would question whether this is a strategy paper at all: right now it describes how audiences judge music, with no strategic choice by the producer, no implications for performance or value capture, and no way to rule out that quality or label promotion explains the results.
- *[theory]* **Make the artist's or label's positioning choice the focus. Model differentiation as a strategic decision with trade-offs between audiences, and connect the reactions of radio stations and critics to later commercial performance and career survival.**: SMJ publishes work on firms' strategic choices and their performance consequences, not just on how audiences evaluate products.
- *[methods]* **Address endogeneity and quality confounds. Possible tools are artist fixed effects, within-artist changes, label promotion controls, and an exogenous shock, such as a change in radio consolidation or playlist algorithms, that shifts how much each audience matters.**: SMJ reviewers expect credible identification, and promotion budgets or unobserved quality could produce the same pattern of radio and critic preferences.
- *[positioning]* **Present the contribution as a theory of optimal distinctiveness across multiple audiences: explain when producers can or should satisfy commercial and legitimacy-granting audiences at the same time, and how status or prior critical acclaim changes this.**: Tying the paper to the optimal distinctiveness and category literatures already published in SMJ makes the findings generalizable beyond music.

**Management Science**: fit 30/100. We would value the careful measurement of objective (sonic) versus subjective (genre) differentiation along with the contrast between commercial and legitimating audiences, but we would question whether correlations between differentiation and plays or reviews identify causal audience preferences rather than selection by artists and labels, and what the results mean for firms' positioning strategy.
- *[methods]* **Use quasi-exogenous variation to identify audience responses separately from artist and label selection, for example through release-timing shocks to which chart toppers are contemporaneous, or within-album comparisons of single choices.**: Management Science expects causal identification, and right now differentiation is chosen by artists anticipating how audiences will react.
- *[theory]* **Formalize the trade-off with a simple model of a producer choosing differentiation when facing two audiences with divergent payoffs (commercial gatekeepers and legitimacy-granting critics), then derive and test predictions about optimal positioning across career stages.**: Linking empirics to a tractable decision model turns a description of audience tastes into a contribution to positioning and differentiation strategy that the journal's readers can generalize.
- *[practice]* **Trace downstream outcomes such as streams, sales, label contract renewal and later releases to quantify how much each audience's response contributes to artist performance and repositioning.**: Showing the economic consequences of critic versus radio reactions establishes managerial relevance beyond audience-level correlations.

## A Note on “Corporate Provision of Public Goods” (Management Science, 2026)

**Organization Science**: fit 3/100. Organization Science would ask what this correction teaches us about organizing. It would not value a technical counterexample to an equilibrium refinement result in a game-theory paper, because the note makes no claim about organizations, managers, or stakeholders that the journal's community could build on.
- *[positioning]* **Recast the counterexample as a theory of when firms led by managers who serve shareholders fail to mobilize capital for socially valuable ventures. Spell out the organizational conditions (governance, manager discretion, what investors believe about each other's motives) under which funding breaks down.**: Organization Science publishes formal models only when they produce insight about organizations, as with the papers on political conflict and learning or on CEO belief misalignment, and not when they correct an equilibrium proof.
- *[positioning]* **Link the model to hybrid organizing and corporate purpose. Ask how firms that bundle private returns with public-good provision compete with bonds or other investment channels for the money of citizen-investors.**: This would connect the result to active conversations in the journal on social enterprise trade-offs, prosocial motives, and stakeholder theory.
- *[data]* **Pair the formal analysis with empirical evidence or a calibrated simulation, for example data on social-impact ventures that fail to raise funding or a lab experiment on how investors choose to fund prosocial firms.**: The journal values formal theory that produces testable, behaviorally grounded predictions and, ideally, evidence that the mechanism actually operates.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 3/100. We would ask what this tells strategy scholars: we publish formal models (value capture, property rights, relational contracts) when they change how we think about firm strategy, value creation, or stakeholder governance, and a counterexample note correcting an equilibrium claim in another journal's paper doesn't do that.
- *[theory]* **Turn the counterexample into a characterization: find the economic conditions (preferences, bond returns, how efficient the firm is at providing the public good) under which ventures with social goals do or do not get funded.**: Our formal articles add value by clearly mapping when firms beat other ways of providing social goods, as in our published work comparing CSR by for-profit firms with nonprofits.
- *[positioning]* **Frame the analysis around a strategy question: when can a profit-seeking firm acting for shareholders attract capital to provide public goods, compared with bonds, nonprofits, or hybrid organizations?**: Linking the model to the CSR, stakeholder-governance and comparative-governance debates in this journal would give it a real audience and a theoretical contribution.
- *[theory]* **Draw testable propositions about when funding fails, and if possible illustrate them with data from impact investing, green bonds, or crowdfunded social ventures.**: Readers here expect formal theory to produce predictions that empirical strategy research can test.

**Management Science**: fit 35/100. Management Science does publish comments that correct a result in one of its own articles, but the board will ask whether the counterexample is more than a technicality: does it overturn the original paper's managerial message about when firms should provide public goods, or does it only show that a refinement-dependent claim fails at the edges?
- *[theory]* **Go beyond showing that counterexamples exist: characterize the full set of economies (preferences, number of citizens, return on the venture, bond yield) in which no-funding equilibria survive trembling-hand perfection, and identify exactly which step of the original proof fails.**: A necessary-and-sufficient characterization turns a narrow correction into a usable boundary condition that later work can build on.
- *[methods]* **Find out whether the no-funding equilibria survive stronger refinements, such as proper equilibrium, strict or robust equilibria, or evolutionary stability, and how likely they are to be selected.**: Readers will want to know whether the coordination failure is economically plausible or just an artifact of the refinement concept.
- *[practice]* **Spell out what the result means for corporate social responsibility and impact investing, for example whether mechanisms like commitment, minimum-funding thresholds, or managerial mandates restore funding in every equilibrium.**: Linking the theory back to managerial and policy design is what makes a note matter to Management Science's broad readership.
