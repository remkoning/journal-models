# Same paper, three journals (rulebook)

## Tertius Dolens : Interalter Conflict and Its Negative Impact on Broker Performance (Organization Science, 2026)

**Organization Science**: fit 58/100. We value how the paper turns brokerage from an advantage into a liability when the alters are in conflict, which fits the paradox-and-contingency theorizing we prize, but we question whether the reputational and meaning-making mechanism is actually theorized and separated from plain coordination costs, or whether the paper mostly estimates a network-performance effect for strategy readers.
- *[mechanism]* **Theorize how third-party audiences, such as other partners and potential alters, read the broker's ties to feuding firms (as taking sides, unreliable or compromised), and test this reputational path separately from the knowledge-flow and coordination paths, for example by looking at new tie formation and partner exits after conflicts start.**: A theory of how audiences interpret the broker's ties is central to this journal, and separating mechanisms is what lifts the paper beyond a moderated performance regression.
- *[methods]* **Use conflict onsets that are external to the broker (such as patent litigation or antitrust disputes between alters) as a quasi-experiment, and model the broker's later responses: dissolving a tie, rebalancing toward one alter, or deepening multiplex ties.**: This ties the broker's decisions to organizational structure over time and addresses the concern that conflict-exposed brokers are selected rather than harmed.
- *[theory]* **Ground the moderators in named mechanisms: status as a buffer of legitimacy, multiplexity as commitment and trust (informal control), and conflict-free structural holes as a way to diversify across relationships. Also connect the paper to Simmelian tie and balance theory and to the tertius iungens / tertius gaudens debate.**: Moderators derived from theory, which settle a tension between brokerage theory and the evidence on conflict, speak to core debates in organization theory.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 68/100. We value the firm-level outcome (innovation performance), the clear contingency logic, and the fact that it names the costs of a network position. We would question whether the abstract makes clear what the empirical evidence is, how conflict is measured, and what brokers should do strategically.
- *[methods]* **State the empirical design explicitly. For example, use alliance network panel data with interalter conflict measured through litigation, such as patent suits between alters, and patent-based innovation outcomes, and address the endogeneity of brokerage positions.**: SMJ requires hypotheses tested with archival evidence, and the abstract currently reads as purely theoretical.
- *[positioning]* **Frame the tertius dolens position as a strategic choice with trade-offs. Examine whether brokers respond by dissolving ties, choosing a side or restructuring their portfolios, and what that costs them.**: Treating the network position as a firm decision with costs and remedies links the paper to corporate and alliance strategy and gives managers clear actions.
- *[positioning]* **Anchor the mechanisms in core strategy theory, such as the knowledge-based view and competitive dynamics, and claim a contribution to the alliance portfolio and competitive strategy literatures.**: An explicit contribution to strategy literatures keeps the paper from reading as a sociological network study.

**Management Science**: fit 18/100. We'd value a causal answer to whether conflict between a firm's partners turns brokerage into a cost for its innovation, but as written the paper leans on a coined label ("tertius dolens") and a verbally theorized list of moderators, with no identification strategy and no effect sizes.
- *[methods]* **Use exogenous shocks to conflict between alters, such as patent litigation or antitrust suits that start between two partners, in an event-study or difference-in-differences design comparing brokers with matched non-brokers.**: Credible causal identification would separate the effect of conflict from the selection of firms into risky network positions, which panel associations cannot do.
- *[theory]* **Replace the verbal theory with a simple model of a broker choosing how to share knowledge between rival alters who may leak or appropriate it, and derive testable predictions about which outside options (other partners, status, multiplex ties) soften the loss.**: A crisp economic mechanism based on appropriation risk and outside options would replace jargon and a list of moderators with predictions the data can test.
- *[mechanism]* **Report magnitudes in interpretable units, such as the percentage change in patents or citation-weighted patents after the conflict begins, and test the proposed channels directly: knowledge flows measured by co-citation between the broker and each alter, and changes in who the broker partners with.**: Effect sizes and direct evidence on the channels show managers how costly this exposure is and which of the proposed mechanisms actually operates.

## Shaping Ecosystem Rules: Complementarities, Interdependencies, and Firms’ Success in Coordinating Ecosystems Via Standard-Setting (Organization Science, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 68/100. We value the socio-technical trade-off, where relational ties both help and hurt depending on how central a proposal is. We question whether "adjustment costs" stays an economic-efficiency story unless the paper theorizes how partners and the committee read core proposals as threats to legitimacy, identity or reciprocity.
- *[mechanism]* **Recast the mechanism as relational obligation versus reciprocity breach: core proposals put partners' commitments at risk, so they withdraw support or switch to symbolic support. Test this by separating explicit opposition from quiet non-endorsement.**: This moves the contribution from cost-based logic to social meaning-making and relational dynamics, which are central to OS.
- *[scope]* **Build moderators from named mechanisms, such as the proposer's status or perceived neutrality, how embedded its ties are (multiplex versus transactional), and whether partners hold complementary or substitute components.**: Theory-derived contingencies would show when relational influence survives a proposal's technical centrality, rather than only reporting a main-effect trade-off.
- *[theory]* **Link the analysis to the SSO's formal decision structure (working-group chairs, voting versus consensus rules, editorship). Show how these formal arrangements reinforce or offset informal relational influence, using within-firm variation across proposals.**: Bringing formal and informal governance together ties the study to core organization-theory conversations, beyond a strategy framing about ecosystems.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 74/100. We value that this treats a firm's attempts to shape ecosystem rules as a strategic choice and names a clear socio-technical trade-off: relational influence helps, but core proposals impose adjustment costs on partners. We would question whether "shaping success" ever reaches firm-level value, and whether the evidence and the contingencies are spelled out clearly.
- *[data]* **Connect shaping success to firm-level outcomes: licensing revenue from standard-essential patents, product market share, or the firm's later position in the ecosystem.**: SMJ wants the strategic choice tied to value creation and competitive advantage, not just to whether a proposal gets accepted.
- *[scope]* **Test contingencies such as whether ties go to direct partners or rivals, how vertically integrated the firm is, and whether the firm offers side-payments or modular fallback options that lower partners' adjustment costs.**: Showing when the trade-off gets stronger or weaker gives managers practical advice and fits SMJ's focus on how fit and firm characteristics change the main relationship.
- *[positioning]* **Name the data and design (for example, 3GPP contribution-level archival data, with identification of a proposal's technical centrality) and state the contribution to complementor and ecosystem strategy.**: Clear archival evidence grounded in core theory (capabilities and interdependence, plus relational resources) and an explicit claim to the strategy literature are what reviewers expect.

**Management Science**: fit 35/100. The board values the explicit trade-off: relational influence lowers the cost of passing proposals, while core proposals impose adjustment costs on partners. That is an economic mechanism we care about. We question whether panel associations from standards bodies can separate influence from proposal quality and selection, and the abstract gives no effect sizes.
- *[theory]* **Formalize the trade-off in a simple voting or bargaining model. Partners weigh the benefit of supporting an ally against the adjustment costs that depend on how central a proposal is. Derive comparative statics you can test.**: A crisp model would turn the verbal 'socio-technical trade-off' into predictions that follow from incentives, which Management Science rewards.
- *[methods]* **Exploit exogenous variation in ties or in proposal centrality as a source of causal identification. Candidate sources are mergers that create or remove ties, personnel moves, or changes in how working groups are assigned.**: Credible identification would rule out the possibility that well-connected firms simply write better or more strategic proposals.
- *[practice]* **Measure partners' adjustment costs directly with contribution-level data, such as the products or code each partner must change. Report effect sizes as changes in acceptance probability per added tie at different levels of centrality.**: Interpretable magnitudes and a directly measured mechanism make the managerial implications concrete for firms choosing which proposals to push.

## Firm growth and stagnation in the United States: Key trends and new data opportunities (Strategic Management Journal, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 6/100. The dataset itself is impressive, but Organization Science would ask what theory it explains: a new measure plus descriptive trends, written for strategy readers, doesn't yet offer a mechanism about how organizations learn, search or get evaluated.
- *[mechanism]* **Pick one theoretical puzzle, such as why stagnant firms keep growing in number. Use BDS-HG to test, decided in advance, between two competing mechanisms: competency traps or exploitation lock-in (organizational learning) versus the founder's identity or lifestyle-oriented goals (aspiration-level theory).**: The journal publishes work that settles a tension between mechanisms, not datasets or descriptions of trends.
- *[data]* **Link the BDS-HG cells to Census microdata (LBD or the Annual Business Survey) to see how organizational structure affects whether young firms move from high growth into stagnation. Examples of structure include founder team composition, when formal hierarchy is added, and multi-unit expansion.**: Connecting firm-level structure and growth processes to how growth plays out over time is a core organization-theory question that the aggregate data can't answer by itself.
- *[theory]* **Theorize the trade-offs of high growth. For example, use attention-based or learning theory to argue that fast scaling helps survival early on but makes firms more likely to shrink later. Build moderators from named mechanisms, not just from industry or region dummies.**: Countervailing effects with stated contingencies are what turn descriptive growth patterns into a theoretical contribution.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 55/100. We value a public firm-growth dataset that lets strategy researchers benchmark their samples and study growth, stagnation and decline, but we ask whether the paper links those trends to strategic choices and core strategy theory rather than mainly describing macro business dynamics.
- *[positioning]* **Frame the three applications around strategy theories, for example real options for scaling decisions by young firms, the resource-based view for persistent stagnation, and competitive dynamics for how incumbents' concentration relates to entrants' growth.**: SMJ wants data papers to clearly push forward strategy literatures, not only macro or economics debates about declining dynamism.
- *[scope]* **Add contingency analyses that test how the decline in high-growth firms and the rise in stagnant firms vary with industry characteristics such as R&D intensity, concentration and technological shocks.**: Showing that environmental and industry conditions shape firm growth turns description into strategy–environment fit questions that SMJ readers care about.
- *[positioning]* **Turn the benchmarking use into a worked demonstration: compare widely used strategy samples (Compustat, VC-backed startups, Crunchbase) against the BDS-HG distribution and show where conclusions may fail to generalize.**: A concrete reassessment of how well influential firm-level evidence generalizes fits SMJ's interest in re-examinations that guide strategy researchers.

**Management Science**: fit 30/100. We value the large administrative data on actual firm behavior and the new reusable measure, but we question a contribution that rests mostly on a data release, descriptive trends and a list of "research opportunities" for strategy scholars, with no mechanism and no causal answer.
- *[mechanism]* **Use BDS-HG to test an economic explanation for the drop in high-growth firms and the rise in stagnant firms, such as weaker reallocation from rising adjustment costs, market concentration or lower responsiveness to productivity shocks. Identify the effect using state or industry policy shocks, for example changes in noncompete enforcement, occupational licensing or credit-supply shocks.**: Management Science rewards credible causal mechanisms over documentation of trends.
- *[methods]* **Report the trends in interpretable units. Break the decline into within-cell changes and composition shifts by age, size and industry, and quantify how much of the slowdown in aggregate job creation each component explains.**: Interpretable effect sizes show readers how much each factor matters, so the trends become more than descriptive.
- *[data]* **Show that the measure works by testing whether cell-level growth distributions predict real outcomes, such as later productivity, entry or local employment. Also measure how much commonly used research samples (Compustat, VentureXpert, Crunchbase) differ from the population as a benchmark for selection bias.**: A validated, reusable measure with clear managerial and research consequences fits the journal far better than a list of possible applications.

## Absolute, average‐based, and rank‐based aspirations (Strategic Management Journal, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 62/100. We value a tractable formal model of problemistic search that challenges the received view that optimal aspirations fall as turbulence rises, but we would ask whether "optimal aspiration" and the managerial target-setting framing slide toward incentive design, and we would want the social-comparison mechanism behind the reversal theorized in behavioral terms.
- *[theory]* **Present social versus historical or absolute aspiration specification as a tension in the behavioral theory of the firm. Derive explicit propositions about the mechanism, for example how relative referents move with peers' performance under turbulence and so recalibrate the search trigger on their own.**: OS rewards contributions that settle a conflict between prevailing theory and new reasoning, and it wants the mechanism stated, not only a comparative static.
- *[theory]* **Extend the model so that the comparison group is chosen inside the organization's structure, such as peers within a hierarchy or team, rival units, or a group whose composition shifts. Show how the comparison group's structure and how similar its members are affect exploration across the group.**: Linking individual search rules to group processes and formal structure ties the work to OS conversations on organizational learning and social comparison.
- *[theory]* **Model countervailing effects of rank-based aspirations: more individual exploration but correlated or duplicated search, or gains in the short run but losses in the long run. Specify where these trade-offs reverse, and outline a planned empirical illustration, such as field or experimental data on relative-performance feedback.**: Hidden trade-offs and clear boundary conditions make a model more than a prescription for setting targets, and they connect it to empirical OS work.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 42/100. We value that the paper builds on behavioral theory of the firm, specifically problemistic search and aspirations, and that it speaks to a central strategy tension (exploration versus exploitation) with clear implications for target setting; we would question whether a purely formal model of optimal aspirations, with no empirical test and individual-level searchers, connects clearly enough to firm-level strategic outcomes and contingencies.
- *[methods]* **Test the model's main prediction with data: use archival panel data (e.g., firms benchmarking against industry rank vs. industry average, such as in airline, banking or pharma R&D) or a managerial lab experiment, and check whether rank-based versus average-based social aspirations change how turbulence affects exploration and long-run firm performance.**: SMJ is wary of formal models that derive optimal policies without empirical evidence, so tying the predictions to observed firm search and performance would make the paper a strategy contribution rather than a modeling exercise.
- *[positioning]* **Move the unit of analysis to the firm or business unit: model firms as the searchers whose social aspirations are set against competitors, and add competitive dynamics, since rivals' search changes the reference point.**: Firm-level outcomes and competitive interdependence are what tie the model to competitive strategy and behavioral-theory debates.
- *[scope]* **Name the trade-offs and the boundary conditions: spell out the costs of over-exploration caused by rank-based targets, and how firm characteristics (slack, capabilities, size) and industry features (concentration, number of rivals) shift the optimal aspiration.**: SMJ rewards contingency arguments and clear statements of costs, which also make the managerial advice on target setting concrete.

**Management Science**: fit 62/100. We value the tractable formal model, which derives a sharp optimal-choice result: how the optimal aspiration responds to turbulence depends on whether aspirations are absolute, rank-based or average-based. We would question whether the result holds outside the model's specific assumptions, and whether it has been tied to any data or practical target-setting rule.
- *[theory]* **Extend the model to strategic interaction among agents: let peers' search choices affect one another and solve for the equilibrium aspiration, both when managers set it and when individuals choose it.**: Rank-based aspirations make agents depend on each other's performance, so an equilibrium and incentive analysis would turn a simulation-style result into a crisp economic insight.
- *[methods]* **Test the predicted interaction between turbulence and aspiration type in a field or lab experiment, or in sales-force or platform data. Randomize whether targets are absolute or relative (rank or average) and vary environmental volatility.**: Management Science strongly favors formal predictions that are confirmed with causal evidence on actual behavior.
- *[practice]* **Derive closed-form comparative statics and explicit design rules for target setting, such as how much the optimal relative target should change per unit of turbulence, and quantify the performance lost when the wrong aspiration type is used.**: Interpretable magnitudes and decision-relevant tools make the paper useful to managers designing quotas, rankings and tournaments.

## Play it Again, Sam? Reference-Point Formation and Product Differentiation in the Music Industry (Management Science, 2025)

**Organization Science**: fit 55/100. We value the core idea: different audiences (commercial gatekeepers versus legitimacy-granting critics) judge the same differentiation in opposite ways. We would question whether the paper explains how audiences make sense of objective versus subjective identity, or whether it mostly maps out preferences.
- *[theory]* **Frame the study around optimal distinctiveness and category theory, and state ahead of time, from theory, why the market logic of radio and the field-level legitimacy logic of critics reverse the value of novelty against different reference points (the artist's own past work, peers, chart toppers).**: This moves the paper from describing preference differences to resolving a theoretical tension that sits at the center of OS conversations.
- *[mechanism]* **Test whether the gap between objective identity (sound) and claimed identity (genre labels) matters in its own right. For example, does sounding novel while claiming a familiar genre (or the reverse) lead to authenticity rewards or penalties that differ by audience?**: Comparing what a release is with what it claims to be would explain how audiences read claims and categories to form evaluations, which OS values.
- *[scope]* **Build moderators from named mechanisms, such as artist status, label type (major vs. independent), or critics' organizational position, and plan to test whether critical acclaim at one stage feeds into or offsets radio penalties later.**: Contingencies and cross-stage trade-offs grounded in status and legitimacy would strengthen the paper's organizational mechanism.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 22/100. We value the setting, where the same differentiation choice by an artist gets opposite reactions from commercial and legitimating audiences, but as written the paper is about how evaluators react rather than about the artist's or label's positioning choice and what it means for firm-level outcomes and trade-offs.
- *[theory]* **Make the artist or label the strategic decision-maker. Treat a release's distance from its own catalog, its peers and the chart toppers as a positioning choice, and link it to firm-level outcomes like streams, revenue, chart longevity and later contract or label value.**: SMJ needs a strategic choice with firm-level performance consequences, not just audience preferences.
- *[theory]* **Present the radio-versus-critic split as a trade-off between commercial returns and legitimacy. Draw on optimal distinctiveness and competitive positioning theory, and test whether legitimacy from critics later turns into commercial value or protection from risk.**: Naming the costs and trade-offs of differentiation, and grounding them in core strategy theory, is central to SMJ contributions.
- *[scope]* **Add contingencies such as artist status or career stage, major versus independent label, how crowded the genre is, and periods of market change (for example, the shift to streaming). Show where differentiation pays off and where it is penalized.**: Showing how fit between strategy and environment shapes the main effect will lift the paper from a descriptive account of audiences to a contribution to strategy theory.

**Management Science**: fit 30/100. We value the large multi-source data on how two kinds of evaluators actually behave, and the reusable measure of sonic differentiation, but we question the legitimacy-based framing, the lack of causal identification, and the absence of effect sizes you can interpret.
- *[theory]* **Reframe the radio-versus-critic contrast as a problem of incentives and information: radio stations maximize listener retention and advertising revenue when demand is uncertain, while critics compete for reputation by signaling discovery. Derive the opposite preferences from a simple model of evaluators' objectives.**: Management Science rewards economic mechanisms and formal predictions over appeals to cultural legitimacy.
- *[methods]* **Identify the effects using plausibly exogenous variation in differentiation, such as sudden shifts in what tops the charts (for example, surprise breakout hits) or within-artist comparisons around label or producer changes, and report effects in interpretable units like the percentage change in airplay or review score per standard-deviation change in differentiation.**: Credible identification and interpretable magnitudes are what turn associations into findings this journal can publish.
- *[practice]* **Validate the audio-feature differentiation measure as a reusable tool by showing that it predicts downstream outcomes such as streams, sales, and the artist's later career, and examine how labels and artists strategically choose how much to differentiate given these divergent evaluator demands.**: A validated measure plus the decision-maker's response gives concrete managerial implications for release strategy.

## A Note on “Corporate Provision of Public Goods” (Management Science, 2026)

**Organization Science**: fit 3/100. We would question whether a note that offers a counterexample to an equilibrium-refinement result in a public-goods game advances any organizational mechanism. As it stands, it is a technical economics comment written for the original journal's audience, though the idea that shareholder-oriented firms can be vehicles for prosocial contribution does touch on legitimacy and identity questions we care about.
- *[positioning]* **Recast the question as one about organizational decision processes: how a manager reads shareholders' and stakeholders' prosocial expectations and weighs them when choosing corporate contributions to public goods. Model this as bounded search or learning rather than as equilibrium selection.**: Organization Science accepts formal models when they produce propositions about organizational processes. It does not publish refinement counterexamples.
- *[theory]* **Theorize countervailing effects. For example, prosocial ventures may gain legitimacy and attract funding at the investment stage but crowd out bondholders' private contributions at the giving stage. Specify contingencies such as investor identity, or how much managers are monitored compared with how much they are guided by shared values.**: Trade-offs between formal and normative controls and crowding out are central to the journal's conversations.
- *[mechanism]* **Test the mechanisms empirically with an experiment where participants choose between funding a firm or buying bonds and then decide on contributions. Vary manager discretion and how the firm's purpose is framed to separate legitimacy-based funding from strategic free-riding.**: Evidence that separates mechanisms would turn a theoretical existence result into a contribution about how audiences evaluate firms with a social purpose.

**Strategic Management Journal**: fit 4/100. We would question this note: it is a purely formal counterexample about equilibrium refinement in a public-goods funding game, and it has no empirical test, no core strategy theory and no firm-level strategic outcome, which falls squarely under our red flags for formal-model and economics framing.
- *[positioning]* **Restate the venture-funding decision as a strategic choice by the founder or firm about prosocial positioning, such as tying products to a public good, and draw hypotheses from strategy theory (resource-based view, stakeholder theory) about when that positioning raises the firm's chances of being funded.**: SMJ needs a firm-level strategic choice and a strategy-theory contribution, not just an equilibrium property.
- *[scope]* **Test the conditions the counterexample identifies (for example, the mix of investor preferences and how productive the public good is) using archival data on crowdfunded or impact ventures, with funding success, growth and survival as outcomes.**: Empirical tests of contingencies that affect firm-level outcomes are central to what SMJ publishes.
- *[practice]* **Spell out the trade-offs and managerial implications: when does a mission-driven positioning cost a venture its funding compared with a conventional bond or donation alternative, and what should founders do about it?**: SMJ values explicit costs of strategic moves and clear advice for managers.

**Management Science**: fit 30/100. We value a correction to a game-theoretic result published in Management Science, because getting equilibrium predictions about crowdfunding and public goods right matters. A bare counterexample has a narrow audience, though, unless it identifies which economic conditions decide whether the venture gets funded.
- *[theory]* **Go beyond existence and characterize the parameter regions (returns, preferences, number of citizens, bond terms) in which the venture is or is not funded in every trembling-hand perfect equilibrium.**: A characterization delivers a clear economic insight and testable predictions, which a single counterexample does not.
- *[mechanism]* **Explain the economic mechanism behind the failure, such as coordination, free-riding by bondholders, or the incentives the manager faces when acting for shareholders, and say how it changes the original paper's welfare conclusions.**: Management Science readers care about incentives and trade-offs, not only about refining an equilibrium concept.
- *[practice]* **Draw out implications for platform or policy design, for example how to structure bonds or subsidies so that funding happens, and describe how these could be tested with crowdfunding or lab data.**: Tying the result to real decision makers and empirical tests makes it relevant to managers and policymakers.
