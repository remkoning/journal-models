## What Management Science values
1. Formal models (game-theoretic, principal-agent, dynamic, market equilibrium) that derive clear results about incentives, contracts, or welfare.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, contractibility, credibility, market failure, efficiency, collusion, or the costs and benefits of adoption.
3. Large-scale archival, administrative, platform, census, or proprietary transaction data analysed with careful identification or natural experiments.
4. Precise measurement of an important phenomenon, such as how widespread, fast, or large it is, even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity or policy.
5. Concrete quantified findings (percentages, effect sizes, profit or time gaps) reported directly in the abstract.
6. Controlled lab or field experiments, especially when combined with computational or formal modeling to separate mechanisms.
7. Comparing decision structures (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
8. Outcomes that can be measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, hiring, profits, productivity, sourcing, market share) rather than identities or perceptions.
9. Separating competing explanations or sources, such as supply vs. demand or aggregation vs. context effects, to locate where an effect really comes from.
10. A model or analysis that explains empirical patterns and draws out unexpected implications for value creation or policy.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or a single longitudinal case study that builds a process model or new theory.
2. Methodological commentary or recommendations aimed at researchers in one field rather than a substantive finding.
3. Coining a new concept or label as the headline contribution rather than a model result or a quantified finding.
4. Abstracts mostly made of 'we argue/theorize/hypothesize' statements with no results or magnitudes reported.
5. Framed mainly as extending a named management theory (resource dependence, behavioral theory of the firm, identity) or contributing to a literature.
6. Main focus on how people subjectively experience status, gender, or identity, measured through felt appropriateness or beliefs.
7. Theorizing propositions after the fact from observational patterns ('we propose a self-limiting nature') instead of testing or modeling them.
