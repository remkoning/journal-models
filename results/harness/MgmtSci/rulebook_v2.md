## What Management Science values
1. Formal analytical models (game-theoretic, equilibrium, optimization) yielding conditions under which a decision or design choice is optimal are strongly favored.
2. Models framed in economic terms (competition, incentives, welfare, platform or contract design) beat models framed around sociological concepts like legitimacy or stratification.
3. Explicit causal identification: randomized or quasi-random assignment, natural experiments, unexpected shocks such as deaths, instruments, or fixed-effects designs isolating exogenous variation.
4. Tests a clearly specified economic or behavioral mechanism and explicitly rules out rival explanations, e.g., signaling, selection, or anticipatory departure.
5. Reports economically interpretable magnitudes, such as percentile-equivalent or percentage effects, rather than only significance and direction of association.
6. Uses observed behavior in real markets or organizations (sales, revenues, ratings, performance, transactions) at scale rather than hypothetical choices.
7. Directly tests a well-known economic theory or a common practitioner claim against data, even with a surprising or null result.
8. Draws implications for how organizations, platforms, or evaluation processes should be designed, or for consumer welfare and policy.
9. Combines a theoretical model with several datasets, or separates individual-level from team-level effects using appropriate estimation.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Contribution framed as bridging literatures, combining theoretical perspectives, or 'extending research on X' rather than answering a sharp question.
2. Hypothesis-driven contingency stories stacking moderators (life stage, hierarchy level, orientation, tenure) on associational archival correlations without identification.
3. Purely conceptual pieces, literature reviews, or commentary on modeling approaches without new formal results or evidence.
4. Vignette or online experiments testing a theorized organizational paradox or perspective, where field data on market behavior would be expected instead.
5. Institution-centric or organization-theory constructs (institutional factors, exploration, authenticity) as the main contribution, with mechanisms asserted rather than identified.
6. Strategy-journal framing aimed at firms' competitive positioning ('informs complementor strategy') rather than mechanism, design, or welfare.
