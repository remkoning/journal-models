## What Management Science values
1. Economics-style mechanisms such as incentives, information asymmetry, signaling, matching, equilibrium, or explicit trade-offs, rather than legitimacy, identity, or institutional logics.
2. Reports effect sizes in interpretable units (percentages, per-unit responses, gap closed), not just support for hypotheses' direction.
3. Credible causal identification (random or quasi-random assignment, unexpected deaths or shocks, instruments, policy changes) decisively beats panel associations with moderators, even with richer theory.
4. A formal model (real options, matching, game, optimal choice) deriving predictions tested on field or archival data, ideally several datasets, even if magnitudes are unstated.
5. A new, validated, reusable measure that predicts real outcomes; not a critique or correction of an existing measure.
6. Large-sample archival, administrative, platform, or field data on actual behavior over small samples, vignettes, or single cases.
7. Shows how decision makers (firms, employers, evaluators, boards) actually respond to constraints, with concrete managerial or policy implications.
8. Precise, decision-relevant formalization of fuzzy strategy concepts that produces tools usable in empirical work.
9. On sociological or audience-perception topics, prefer large-scale behavioral evidence from archival or platform data over experiments, qualitative, or multimethod case work.
10. Questions about bias, discrimination, or inequality framed as testable demand-side or incentive mechanisms rather than as contextualizing institutional forces.
11. Traces an observed gap to a specific channel by explicitly ruling out competing explanations (selection, signaling, enforcer bias, anticipation).
12. Studies innovation as search or problem-solving behavior measured on novel behavioral data, rather than audience perceptions of novelty or distinctiveness.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Inductive qualitative work: grounded theory, single in-depth case studies, ethnography, or process models built from interviews.
2. Contribution is a named literature or coined label (a 'paradox', named hypothesis, optimal distinctiveness) itself, not an identified, tested effect.
3. Long verbal theorizing ('we/I theorize', inverted U, moderation by life stage or structure, bridging perspectives) where data merely confirm directions without identification.
4. Formal models full of coined jargon constructs and long lists of propositions, without a crisp economic insight or empirical tie-in.
5. Purely conceptual critique or redefinition of a construct with only verbal or illustrative arguments.
6. Context-bound descriptive findings (one occupation or sector) framed as institutional or contextual nuance rather than a general mechanism.
7. Lab, online, or vignette experiments on stylized tasks or hypothetical perceptions, when the rival uses field randomization or real behavioral data.
8. Methodological critique of an existing measure pitched as guidance for 'organizational' or strategy scholars rather than answering a substantive question.
