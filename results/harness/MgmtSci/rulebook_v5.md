## What Management Science values
1. Economics-style mechanisms (incentives, information asymmetry, self-enforcing contracts, signaling, matching, equilibrium, trade-offs) rather than legitimacy, identity, culture, or institutional logics.
2. Reports effect sizes in interpretable units (percentages, per-unit responses, gap closed), not just support for hypotheses' direction.
3. Credible causal identification (policy shocks, historical or sudden events, instruments, randomization) beats panel associations with moderators, even on large samples.
4. Analytical formal model (principal-agent, game, optimal policy) with characterized solutions or sharp predictions; valued even without data or stated magnitudes.
5. Computational or structural models count when fitted to experimental or field behavior to separate competing mechanisms, not as stand-alone simulations.
6. A new, validated, reusable measure (e.g., text-based, experimentally checked) that predicts real outcomes; not a critique or correction of an existing measure.
7. Large-sample archival, administrative, platform, or field data on actual behavior, but sharp theory-derived predictions outrank sheer sample size or broad regional proxies.
8. Representative, population-scale measurement of an economically important phenomenon with precise, benchmarked magnitudes counts even without a causal design.
9. Shows how decision makers respond to constraints, or tests a common management practice on real firms, with concrete managerial or policy implications.
10. Precise, decision-relevant formalization of fuzzy strategy concepts that produces tools usable in empirical work.
11. Bias, discrimination, inequality, or welfare questions framed as testable demand-side or incentive mechanisms, including counterintuitive efficiency or welfare effects.
12. In psychology-style pairs, prefer effort or performance in real work groups plus lab replication over many-study packages on lay beliefs or self-reports.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Inductive qualitative work: grounded theory, single in-depth case studies, ethnography, or process models built from interviews.
2. Contribution built on a coined concept or label ('we introduce the concept of', 'what we call', a new paradox, optimal distinctiveness), even with strong design.
3. Long verbal theorizing ('we theorize', inverted U, moderator lists, 'self-limiting' propositions) where data merely confirm predicted directions without identification.
4. Formal models full of coined jargon constructs and long lists of propositions, without a crisp economic insight or empirical tie-in.
5. Purely conceptual critique or redefinition of a construct with only verbal or illustrative arguments.
6. Context-bound descriptive findings (one occupation or sector) framed as institutional or contextual nuance rather than a general mechanism.
7. Multimethod designs that combine a case narrative with hypothetical-vignette experiments to illustrate a perception mechanism.
8. Pure simulation models arguing against a prevailing explanation without data; or associational findings backed only by 'suggestive' mechanism evidence.
