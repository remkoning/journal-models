## What Strategic Management Journal values
1. The firm, or the founder acting for the venture, is the decision-maker, and the abstract studies a strategic choice such as entry, positioning, investment, scope or innovation direction.
2. Outcomes are firm- or unit-level, such as performance, productivity, value capture, survival or returns. Linking a choice to its payoff beats only predicting the choice.
3. Effects depend on contingencies: the abstract shows how strategy–environment fit or firm and industry characteristics strengthen or weaken the main relationship.
4. The abstract names the costs, trade-offs or unintended penalties of strategic moves for the firm, such as change, repositioning, path dependence or departing from tradition.
5. Individual, team or employee-level mechanisms (micro-foundations) are explicitly tied to firm or unit results, such as team composition shaping innovation output.
6. Strategic leaders such as CEOs, boards, top teams, family owners or founders are agents whose traits shape firm strategy, governance or members' choices.
7. Hypotheses come from named strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral/problemistic search, capabilities, strategic human capital, value creation/appropriation) and are tested on archival or field data.
8. The abstract explicitly claims contributions to strategy literatures, such as competitive, corporate, complementor, entrepreneurial or international strategy, and states what managers should do.
9. Customer or audience reactions are framed as determinants of the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice, not as phenomena of evaluator cognition in their own right.
10. How a firm organizes internally, through multi-unit footprint, cross-unit knowledge transfer or team design, explains productivity differences among its units or the firm as a whole.
11. Interactions between firms, such as rivals, bidders or partners, are framed as shaping the focal firm's value capture or competitive position.

## What Strategic Management Journal does not value / red flags
1. A formal or analytical model deriving optimal design, pricing or fee policies without empirical testing suggests an operations, economics or marketing journal.
2. An economics framing, such as market-price tests or effects of public data, policy or collective arrangements on industry-wide aggregates, not on focal-firm choice and performance.
3. Employee mobility, promotion, aspiration or career outcomes explained by network position or labor processes, with no link to firm strategy, founders or performance.
4. A sociological focus on categories, identity or evaluator perception where the producer is an individual and the firm's strategic value is absent.
5. Inductive case-based process theorizing about routines, identity or work practices that is not framed around a strategy construct suggests an organization-theory outlet.
6. Purely conceptual arguments about organizational adaptation, or about reconciling theoretical paradigms, with no empirical or modeled link to strategic outcomes.
7. The main contribution is settling a debate between theories of the firm, rather than explaining a strategic choice and what follows from it.
8. Psychological, group-process or sociological theory used as the main lens, without anchoring in a named strategy construct, suggests another outlet.
