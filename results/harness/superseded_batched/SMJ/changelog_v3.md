**REJECTED** at iteration 3: val accuracy 96.2% (vs OrgSci 95.0%, vs MgmtSci 97.5%); best so far 97.5%, threshold 96.5%. Revision of v1.

Train pool: v1 judged 39 train pairs, accuracy 100.0% (vs OrgSci 100.0%, vs MgmtSci 100.0%). Reflection saw 0 misjudged, 9 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: The outcomes are at firm level: performance, value creation, growth-option value, revenue or competitive advantage, not only individual or audience outcomes.
  - *now*: Outcomes are firm- or unit-level, such as performance, productivity, value capture, survival or returns. Linking a choice to its payoff beats only predicting the choice.
  - *why*: The SMJ articles tied search or strategy choices to performance, while their counterparts only explained antecedents of a behavior such as adaptation.
- **EDITED**: Individual or employee-level mechanisms (micro-foundations) are explicitly tied to firm-level results, such as leaders affecting performance or worker interventions raising revenue.
  - *now*: Individual, team or employee-level mechanisms (micro-foundations) are explicitly tied to firm or unit results, such as team composition shaping innovation output.
  - *why*: SMJ framed team composition inside the firm as micro-foundations of innovation, while the counterpart studied industry-level arrangements.
- **EDITED**: The study covers strategic leadership and governance: top teams, dominant coalitions, family control or CEO succession, and their link to firm strategy or performance feedback.
  - *now*: Strategic leaders such as CEOs, boards, top teams, family owners or founders are agents whose traits shape firm strategy, governance or members' choices.
  - *why*: SMJ published work on founders shaping their joiners' choices. Leadership should cover founders acting as influencers inside their organizations.
- **EDITED**: Hypotheses are derived from core strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral theory of the firm, capabilities, competitive dynamics) and tested on archival or field-experimental data.
  - *now*: Hypotheses come from named strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral/problemistic search, capabilities, strategic human capital, value creation/appropriation) and are tested on archival or field data.
  - *why*: The SMJ winners explicitly named strategic human capital, problemistic search or value creation/appropriation, while their counterparts estimated effects without naming a strategy theory.
- **ADDED**: How a firm organizes internally, through multi-unit footprint, cross-unit knowledge transfer or team design, explains productivity differences among its units or the firm as a whole.
  - *why*: SMJ favored within-firm organization driving unit productivity over industry-wide market-structure effects.
- **ADDED**: Interactions between firms, such as rivals, bidders or partners, are framed as shaping the focal firm's value capture or competitive position.
  - *why*: In the acquisitions pair, SMJ framed contact between firms as reducing bidding competition and raising the acquirer's value; the counterpart took a board group-process view.
- **EDITED**: An economics or finance framing: testing information asymmetry with market-price predictions, macro or local economic outcomes, or impacts at the level of public policy.
  - *now*: An economics framing, such as market-price tests or effects of public data, policy or collective arrangements on industry-wide aggregates, not on focal-firm choice and performance.
  - *why*: The non-SMJ articles estimated how public goods or collective arrangements changed aggregate discovery rates, follow-on innovation or industry structure.
- **EDITED**: Personnel or labor outcomes such as exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, studied without any link to firm strategy or firm performance.
  - *now*: Employee mobility, promotion, aspiration or career outcomes explained by network position or labor processes, with no link to firm strategy, founders or performance.
  - *why*: SMJ accepted career outcomes driven by founder influence in startups, so the flag should target mobility explained purely by labor processes or network position.
- **EDITED**: An inductive comparative case study that builds a process theory from a handful of firms suggests a qualitative organization-theory outlet.
  - *now*: Inductive case-based process theorizing about routines, identity or work practices that is not framed around a strategy construct suggests an organization-theory outlet.
  - *why*: SMJ published a mixed-methods study of a few firms framed around value creation and appropriation, so case methods alone are not disqualifying.
- **EDITED**: Purely conceptual or simulation arguments about organizational adaptation, or about reconciling theoretical paradigms, with no empirical test of strategic outcomes.
  - *now*: Purely conceptual arguments about organizational adaptation, or about reconciling theoretical paradigms, with no empirical or modeled link to strategic outcomes.
  - *why*: SMJ published a network simulation model, so simulation by itself should not be treated as a red flag.
- **ADDED**: Psychological, group-process or sociological theory used as the main lens, without anchoring in a named strategy construct, suggests another outlet.
  - *why*: A counterpart that relied on group information-processing theory lost. The SMJ anchor that borrowed psychology tied it to strategic framing, hence the anchoring condition.
