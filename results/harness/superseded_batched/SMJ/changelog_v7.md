# Changelog v7 (revision of v1)

**REJECTED** at iteration 7: val accuracy 93.8% (vs OrgSci 95.0%, vs MgmtSci 92.5%); best so far 97.5%, threshold 96.5%. Revision of v1.

Train pool: v1 judged 40 train pairs, accuracy 96.2% (vs OrgSci 92.5%, vs MgmtSci 100.0%). Reflection saw 2 misjudged, 7 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: The study covers strategic leadership and governance: top teams, dominant coalitions, family control or CEO succession, and their link to firm strategy or performance feedback.
  - *now*: The study covers strategic leadership and governance, such as top teams, boards, family control or CEO succession, and links it to firm strategy or performance.
  - *why*: Behavioral studies of performance feedback driven by managerial cognition were wrongly preferred over stakeholder-strategy work published in the journal.
- **EDITED**: Hypotheses are derived from core strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral theory of the firm, capabilities, competitive dynamics) and tested on archival or field-experimental data.
  - *now*: Arguments draw on strategy-native theories (resource-based, knowledge-based, real options, capabilities, competitive dynamics, stakeholder strategy) rather than generic organization or psychology theories.
  - *why*: Published articles drew on knowledge-based or stakeholder theory, and some were conceptual or qualitative; requiring a data type misled the board.
- **EDITED**: Customer or audience reactions are framed as determinants of the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice, not as phenomena of evaluator cognition in their own right.
  - *now*: Audience reactions, or entrepreneurs' readings of market meaning, are framed as shaping a firm's strategic choice or its value, not studied as evaluator cognition for its own sake.
  - *why*: A study of category contests shaping entrepreneurs' entry strategies was published in the journal but scored as sociology.
- **ADDED**: Conceptual theory-building, reviews or formal propositions about core strategy constructs, such as capabilities, competitive advantage, real options or rivalry-cooperation, fit the journal even without data.
  - *why*: Conceptual papers on capabilities and on competitive-cooperative dynamics were published in the journal, but the old conceptual red flag penalized them.
- **ADDED**: How the firm configures itself (unit location, scope, structure, or how alliances are spread across units) explains differences in productivity or performance across firms.
  - *why*: Published articles explained performance through internal geographic or structural configuration, unlike same-topic papers on industry-wide or policy effects.
- **ADDED**: Human capital, networks and stakeholder relationships are treated as resources the firm or founder assembles and manages, with effects on growth, innovation or value.
  - *why*: Published articles on startup hiring, founder network activation and employee stakeholder relationships were underweighted relative to finance or practice-transfer counterparts.
- **EDITED**: An economics or finance framing: testing information asymmetry with market-price predictions, macro or local economic outcomes, or impacts at the level of public policy.
  - *now*: An economics or finance framing: investor returns, information asymmetry tested with market prices, effects of public programs, or outcomes at the policy or regional level.
  - *why*: Pairs on investor returns and on effects of public data were judged with low confidence; the red flag needed sharper wording.
- **EDITED**: Personnel or labor outcomes such as exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, studied without any link to firm strategy or firm performance.
  - *now*: Personnel or labor outcomes such as exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, studied without linking them to how firms assemble resources or to firm performance.
  - *why*: A hiring and earnings study framed as startups acquiring human capital belongs to the journal and should not be flagged.
- **EDITED**: A sociological focus on categories, identity or evaluator perception where the producer is an individual and the firm's strategic value is absent.
  - *now*: A sociological focus on categories, labels, identity or evaluator perception, where the unit is a word, product or individual and no firm strategic choice is involved.
  - *why*: A study of category-label adoption (non-journal) was hard to tell apart from identity and category work that the journal published with a strategy link.
- **EDITED**: An inductive comparative case study that builds a process theory from a handful of firms suggests a qualitative organization-theory outlet.
  - *now*: Inductive qualitative work that builds organizational process theory but makes no claim about competitive advantage or strategy suggests an organization-theory outlet.
  - *why*: A grounded-theory single-case study tied to competitive advantage was published in the journal, but the old rule pushed against it.
- **EDITED**: Purely conceptual or simulation arguments about organizational adaptation, or about reconciling theoretical paradigms, with no empirical test of strategic outcomes.
  - *now*: Computational or simulation models of individual decision biases or of organizational adaptation, with no empirical test of firm strategic outcomes.
  - *why*: Conceptual strategy theory is published in the journal, but computational models of entrepreneur bias appeared elsewhere; the flag is narrowed to simulations.
- **EDITED**: The main contribution is settling a debate between theories of the firm, rather than explaining a strategic choice and what follows from it.
  - *now*: The main contribution is settling a debate between theories of the firm or of decision-making, rather than explaining a strategic choice and what follows from it.
  - *why*: The non-journal counterpart framed its contribution as overturning a consensus about decision biases.
- **ADDED**: Stylized laboratory tasks that induce or measure a construct, contributing mainly to learning, transfer or psychology theory rather than to firm strategy.
  - *why*: A lab-game study of a strategy construct that contributed to transfer theory appeared outside the journal and beat the published conceptual paper.
