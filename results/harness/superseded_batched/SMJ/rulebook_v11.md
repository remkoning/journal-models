## What Strategic Management Journal values
1. The firm, or the founder acting for the venture, is the decision-maker, studying a strategic choice: entry, positioning, investment, scope, sourcing, preemptive response or innovation direction.
2. Outcomes are firm-level (performance, value creation, growth, survival, competitive advantage); individual outcomes count only when explained by firm strategy conditions through a named strategy theory.
3. Effects depend on contingencies: the abstract shows how strategy–environment fit or firm and industry characteristics strengthen or weaken the main relationship.
4. The abstract names the costs, trade-offs or unintended penalties of strategic moves for the firm, such as change, repositioning, path dependence or departing from tradition.
5. Multi-unit or multinational firms: how the parent's internal configuration of units or inventor networks shapes knowledge transfer, productivity or innovation inside the firm.
6. Strategic leadership and governance: boards, top teams, family control, CEO or director succession and turnover, explained through behavioral, legitimacy or power dynamics inside the firm.
7. Hypotheses come from strategy theories, tested on archival data or experiments; formal models qualify when they derive core strategy phenomena such as risk–return, rivalry or performance heterogeneity.
8. The abstract uses strategy vocabulary (competitive advantage, strategic human capital, corporate strategy, strategy research) and explicitly states its contribution to strategy literatures.
9. Customer, analyst or other audience reactions count when framed as the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice or change, not as evaluator cognition itself.
10. Behavioral strategy and upper echelons: traits, biases or experience of executives, including those below the CEO, characterize or explain firm decisions and capabilities.
11. Conceptual models or reviews are acceptable when they explain competitive (dis)advantage, persistence of firm heterogeneity, or synthesize a strategy theory stream.
12. Nonmarket strategy: political ties, regulation, policy or crises treated as firm resources or risks whose gain or loss changes firm innovation, performance or business-model viability.

## What Strategic Management Journal does not value / red flags
1. Formal mathematical work that derives optimal contracts or operational policies, with no empirical test and no link to a core strategy phenomenon, suggests an economics or operations outlet.
2. Economics or finance framing where investor or stock-market reactions or aggregate trends are the outcome; exogenous shocks used only for identification are fine.
3. Labor-market or HR-practice studies (hiring, training, mobility, turnover, gender gaps) judged as practice effectiveness or individual careers, not tied to firm strategic choice or firm outcomes.
4. Evaluator focus: the dependent variable is investors', critics', media's or recruiters' judgments as an evaluation phenomenon, unconnected to the cost or value of a firm's strategic move.
5. Organization-theory core (institutions, logics, status, categories, authenticity, population-level learning) as the main contribution, unless explicitly tied to a firm's strategic choice and its costs or advantage.
6. Simulation or conceptual work on organizational adaptation, or settling debates between theories of the firm, that names no strategic choice and tests no strategic outcome.
7. Decisions modeled mainly as learning from external information signals (customer feedback, rival failures) under uncertainty, without firm performance consequences, suggest a management-science or organization-science outlet.
8. Institutional context, public policy or public goods (voids, immigration rules, public data) is the main explanation, with individual careers, industry-wide shares or welfare as outcome and firm choice incidental.
