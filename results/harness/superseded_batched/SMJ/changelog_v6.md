# Changelog v6 (revision of v1)

**REJECTED** at iteration 6: val accuracy 95.0% (vs OrgSci 95.0%, vs MgmtSci 95.0%); best so far 97.5%, threshold 96.5%. Revision of v1.

Train pool: v1 judged 39 train pairs, accuracy 93.6% (vs OrgSci 94.7%, vs MgmtSci 92.5%). Reflection saw 3 misjudged, 6 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: The outcomes are at firm level: performance, value creation, growth-option value, revenue or competitive advantage, not only individual or audience outcomes.
  - *now*: Outcomes are at firm level: performance, innovation, growth, revenue, competitive advantage, or investors' valuation of the firm (e.g., abnormal returns), not only outcomes for individuals or audiences.
  - *why*: The board marked down SMJ articles that measure firm value through stock reactions or measure innovation as the outcome.
- **EDITED**: Effects depend on contingencies: the abstract shows how strategy–environment fit or firm and industry characteristics strengthen or weaken the main relationship.
  - *now*: Effects depend on contingencies: firm resources, competitive intensity, strategy–environment fit or firm ties strengthen or weaken the main relationship.
  - *why*: SMJ winners used moderators such as firm resources, competition and firm ties, not differences in country institutions.
- **EDITED**: Individual or employee-level mechanisms (micro-foundations) are explicitly tied to firm-level results, such as leaders affecting performance or worker interventions raising revenue.
  - *now*: Micro-foundations link levels in either direction: employee or leader mechanisms drive firm results, or firm strategy and growth shape employee outcomes.
  - *why*: SMJ also publishes studies where firm growth dynamics explain employee experience; the old rule only worked in one direction.
- **EDITED**: The study covers strategic leadership and governance: top teams, dominant coalitions, family control or CEO succession, and their link to firm strategy or performance feedback.
  - *now*: Strategic leadership and governance: top teams, CEO traits such as founder status or overconfidence, family control or succession in established firms, even without a performance outcome.
  - *why*: The board missed an SMJ study of executive traits that had no performance outcome.
- **EDITED**: Hypotheses are derived from core strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral theory of the firm, capabilities, competitive dynamics) and tested on archival or field-experimental data.
  - *now*: Theory comes from strategy frameworks (resource-based, real options, Penrosean growth, strategic human capital, stakeholder, upper echelons, competitive dynamics), whatever the empirical method.
  - *why*: SMJ published lab and inductive studies, and a field experiment from another journal fooled the board, so the theory anchor matters more than the method.
- **EDITED**: The abstract explicitly claims contributions to strategy literatures, such as competitive, corporate, complementor, entrepreneurial or international strategy, and states what managers should do.
  - *now*: The abstract explicitly claims contributions to strategy literatures (competitive, corporate, nonmarket, entrepreneurial, international, human capital) or to competitive advantage, and says what managers should do.
  - *why*: SMJ winners framed their contribution around strategic human capital, nonmarket strategy or competitive advantage.
- **EDITED**: Customer or audience reactions are framed as determinants of the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice, not as phenomena of evaluator cognition in their own right.
  - *now*: Customer, investor or audience reactions are framed as determinants of the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice, not as evaluator cognition in their own right.
  - *why*: Investor reactions that value a firm's strategy fit SMJ and should not trigger the finance red flag.
- **ADDED**: Nonmarket strategy: political connections, lobbying, stakeholder or activist relations treated as firm resources or strategies whose value depends on firm actions.
  - *why*: In several pairs, the SMJ side treated political or stakeholder ties as strategic resources; the other side used regulatory-capture or social-movement framing.
- **ADDED**: Lab or field experiments manipulate an organizational or managerial lever (monitoring, incentives, structure, communication) and theorize its effect on strategic behavior such as search.
  - *why*: The SMJ lab study manipulated a managerial intervention; the other journal's lab study measured individual cognitive traits.
- **EDITED**: An economics or finance framing: testing information asymmetry with market-price predictions, macro or local economic outcomes, or impacts at the level of public policy.
  - *now*: Economics or political-science framing: regulatory capture, self-enforcing contract theory, macro or public-policy impacts, with no firm strategy explaining variation in outcomes.
  - *why*: The old rule wrongly penalized an SMJ abnormal-returns study, while relational-contract and regulatory-capture papers went elsewhere.
- **EDITED**: Personnel or labor outcomes such as exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, studied without any link to firm strategy or firm performance.
  - *now*: Personnel or labor outcomes such as exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, studied without any link to firm strategy, firm growth or firm performance.
  - *why*: Employee outcomes tied to firm growth were accepted at SMJ.
- **EDITED**: A sociological focus on categories, identity or evaluator perception where the producer is an individual and the firm's strategic value is absent.
  - *now*: The main contribution goes to sociological literatures (social movements, categories, status, identity) rather than strategy, even when firms are the units studied.
  - *why*: Firm-level papers that contributed mainly to social-movement or status/identity theory were published elsewhere.
- **EDITED**: An inductive comparative case study that builds a process theory from a handful of firms suggests a qualitative organization-theory outlet.
  - *now*: An inductive case study building a process theory for organization theory, with no link to competitive advantage or strategic outcomes, suggests a qualitative organization-theory outlet.
  - *why*: SMJ published a grounded-theory case study that it tied to competitive advantage.
- **EDITED**: Purely conceptual or simulation arguments about organizational adaptation, or about reconciling theoretical paradigms, with no empirical test of strategic outcomes.
  - *now*: Purely conceptual or simulation arguments, or settling a debate between theories of the firm, with no empirical test of a strategic choice and its consequences.
  - *why*: Merged two overlapping red flags to free a slot.
- **DROPPED**: The main contribution is settling a debate between theories of the firm, rather than explaining a strategic choice and what follows from it.
  - *why*: Merged into the conceptual/simulation red flag.
- **ADDED**: Studies of individual aspiring entrepreneurs, their pitches, training or investor funding decisions, rather than firm or executive strategy, suggest an entrepreneurship journal.
  - *why*: The board misjudged a real-options field experiment on would-be entrepreneurs' pitches as SMJ; SMJ took the study of executives in established firms.
- **ADDED**: Explanations centred on country institutions or foreign-versus-domestic firm differences, rather than firm resources or strategies, suggest an international business journal.
  - *why*: An institutions-based foreign-versus-domestic comparison lost to an SMJ paper that explained effects through firm resources and competition.
