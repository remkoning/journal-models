# Changelog v10 (revision of v9)

**ACCEPTED** at iteration 10: val accuracy 97.5% (vs OrgSci 95.0%, vs MgmtSci 100.0%); best so far 97.5%, threshold 96.5%. Revision of v9.

Train pool: v9 judged 40 train pairs, accuracy 95.0% (vs OrgSci 100.0%, vs MgmtSci 90.0%). Reflection saw 2 misjudged, 7 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: The firm, or the founder acting for the venture, is the decision-maker, and the abstract studies a strategic choice such as entry, positioning, investment, scope or innovation direction.
  - *now*: The firm, or the founder acting for the venture, is the decision-maker, studying a strategic choice: entry, positioning, investment, scope, sourcing, preemptive response or innovation direction.
  - *why*: Merged the separate proactive-response rule into this one to make room for a nonmarket-strategy rule.
- **DROPPED**: Firms' proactive or comparative-cost responses to threats or partner changes, such as make-versus-buy shifts or preemptive moves, framed as deliberate strategic choices.
  - *why*: Folded into the first rule; it repeated the strategic-choice principle.
- **EDITED**: Individual outcomes count when tied to firm strategy: individual mechanisms that drive firm search or performance, or employee mobility and spawning caused by firm strategic actions.
  - *now*: Individual-level outcomes count when explained by firm-level strategic conditions (growth, strategy, resource frictions) through a named strategy theory such as Penrosean or resource-based theory.
  - *why*: SMJ published employee-level outcome studies grounded in classic strategy theory over practice-evaluation field experiments that had firm-level sales outcomes.
- **EDITED**: The abstract explicitly claims contributions to strategy literatures, such as competitive, corporate, complementor, entrepreneurial or international strategy, and states what managers should do.
  - *now*: The abstract uses strategy vocabulary (competitive advantage, strategic human capital, corporate strategy, strategy research) and explicitly states its contribution to strategy literatures.
  - *why*: Winners repeatedly framed themselves in strategy terms (strategic human capital, identity-based competitive advantage, strategy research); requiring advice to managers was not discriminating.
- **EDITED**: Behavioral strategy and upper echelons: executive personality, cognition, biases or attention explain firm strategic behavior such as risk taking, CSR, search or competitive response.
  - *now*: Behavioral strategy and upper echelons: traits, biases or experience of executives, including those below the CEO, characterize or explain firm decisions and capabilities.
  - *why*: SMJ took studies documenting executive bias without a performance outcome, and studies of below-CEO executives' experience as microfoundations of capabilities.
- **EDITED**: Reviews or conceptual typologies are acceptable when they synthesize a strategy theory stream or theorize strategies actors use to win resource-holders' support.
  - *now*: Conceptual models or reviews are acceptable when they explain competitive (dis)advantage, persistence of firm heterogeneity, or synthesize a strategy theory stream.
  - *why*: A purely conceptual model on why disadvantage-creating practices persist was SMJ's pick over an empirical organizational-learning study.
- **ADDED**: Nonmarket strategy: political ties, regulation or crises treated as firm resources or risks whose gain or loss changes firm innovation, performance or business-model viability.
  - *why*: SMJ published political-connection and crisis-contagion studies with firm-level outcomes; the board was misreading them as economics framing.
- **EDITED**: Economics or finance framing: capital-market pressures, investor stock-price reactions, exogenous shocks or economy-wide aggregate trends drive outcomes rather than managerial choice.
  - *now*: Economics or finance framing where investor or stock-market reactions or aggregate trends are the outcome; exogenous shocks used only for identification are fine.
  - *why*: The old wording penalized SMJ papers that use exogenous shocks only as a difference-in-differences identification strategy.
- **EDITED**: Rank-and-file HR outcomes such as turnover, promotion, hiring callbacks or gender gaps, studied as labor-market phenomena not caused by firm strategy nor affecting performance.
  - *now*: Labor-market or HR-practice studies (hiring, training, turnover, gender gaps) judged as practice effectiveness, not tied to strategy theory or firm strategic choice.
  - *why*: Field experiments that test HR or training interventions, framed as practice evaluation, appeared outside SMJ.
- **EDITED**: A sociological or infomediary focus where the dependent variable is evaluators' judgment itself, such as media, critic or recruiter perceptions, rather than firm strategic value.
  - *now*: Evaluator focus: the dependent variable is investors', critics', media's or recruiters' judgments of individuals or offerings, rather than firm strategic value.
  - *why*: Studies of investors' decisions on entrepreneurs' pitches belong elsewhere; the rule now names investors explicitly.
- **EDITED**: Inductive or historical case studies contributing mainly to organization theory (institutions, logics, organizing) rather than to strategy process, practice or competitive outcomes.
  - *now*: Organization-theory core (institutions, logics, status, categories, population-level learning) as the main contribution, even with a firm-action outcome, unless explicitly tied to competitive advantage.
  - *why*: SMJ took an inductive case framed around competitive advantage but passed on status/identity and population-learning studies; method was not the dividing line, framing was.
- **EDITED**: Regional, cultural or institutional context is the main explanatory variable and firm choice is incidental, framed as a contextual or macro determinant study.
  - *now*: Institutional or development context (voids, political systems, inclusion) is the main explanatory variable or societal welfare the outcome, with firm choice incidental.
  - *why*: Non-SMJ papers that explain results through institutional features or financial-inclusion welfare beat their SMJ counterparts in the board's judgment.
