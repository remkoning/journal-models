# Changelog v8 (revision of v7)

**REJECTED** at iteration 8: val accuracy 87.5% (vs SMJ 100.0%, vs MgmtSci 73.7%); best so far 91.2%, threshold 90.2%. Revision of v7.

Train pool: v7 judged 39 train pairs, accuracy 97.4% (vs SMJ 94.7%, vs MgmtSci 100.0%). Reflection saw 1 misjudged, 8 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: Quasi-experimental identification (instruments, exogenous shocks, unexpected deaths, quasi-random assignment) foregrounded, or closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions; theory-testing lab or field experiments are not flagged.
  - *now*: Causal identification is the main contribution, with no theorized contingency, and conclusions about aggregate trends, market regulation or policy. Quasi-experiments with theorized moderators are fine.
  - *why*: Several Organization Science articles used sudden deaths, quasi-natural experiments or DiD alongside theorized contingencies; the flag was penalizing them.
- **EDITED**: Headlines a precise effect size, or merely the existence of a bias, penalty or gap, as the core result rather than a theorized organizational process.
  - *now*: Headlines merely the existence of a bias, penalty or gap as the core result, rather than a theorized organizational process or contingency.
  - *why*: Reporting an effect size after a theorized contingency is common in Organization Science articles and does not by itself mark a paper as belonging elsewhere.
- **EDITED**: Game-theoretic or optimal-design models of strategic interaction, incentives or contracting; Organization Science formal models instead derive learning, belief or adaptation dynamics.
  - *now*: Game-theoretic, efficient-contracting, real-option or optimal-design models of rational choice; Organization Science formal models instead derive learning, belief or adaptation dynamics.
  - *why*: Efficient-contract and real-option decision models showed up in the non-Organization Science papers but were not clearly covered.
- **EDITED**: Strategy framing: competitive advantage, firm performance or stock-market returns of strategic moves (acquisitions, brokerage positions), capability microfoundations, strategic human capital, with thin organizational mechanism.
  - *now*: Strategy framing: competitive advantage, prescribing the performance-maximizing choice, returns to strategic moves or portfolio restructuring, capability microfoundations, with thin organizational mechanism.
  - *why*: Non-Organization Science papers framed results as the best choice for performance or as portfolio moves; strategic human capital overlapped with Organization Science founding-team papers.
- **EDITED**: Explains careers, pay, productivity or gaps via individual skills, credentials or labor-market screening, without the organization's relationships, status, structure, power or process.
  - *now*: Explains careers, pay or gaps via individual skills or screening alone; team experience shaping learning, adaptation or decision structure is not flagged.
  - *why*: Organization Science articles on founding-team experience driving learning and adaptation were being wrongly treated as human-capital papers.
- **EDITED**: Reconciles conflicting findings, competing camps or competing views through contingencies built from named theoretical mechanisms, not convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
  - *now*: Resolves a scholarly debate by integrating two named theoretical perspectives or roles into a contingency, whatever the identification strategy, not via convenient proxies.
  - *why*: The misjudged Organization Science article combined two perspectives to settle a debate, but its quasi-experimental design hid that strength from the board.
- **EDITED**: Posits countervailing effects or trade-offs across stages, life cycles or conditions, e.g., a practice or technology shifting costs from one stage to another.
  - *now*: Posits countervailing or curvilinear effects across stages, tenure, life cycles or conditions, e.g., an attribute that helps early but hurts later.
  - *why*: Effects that change with tenure or venture stage marked the Organization Science side in governance and entrepreneurship pairs.
- **EDITED**: Links individual beliefs, cognition or relationships to network structure, formal or informal hierarchy, or group processes such as integration, helping and mobility.
  - *now*: Links individual beliefs or cognition to structural behavior such as networking, hierarchy, integration, helping or mobility, beyond purely intrapsychic outcomes like effort or motivation.
  - *why*: In micro status pairs, the Organization Science article tied beliefs to network behavior, while the other focused on psychological experience and effort.
- **EDITED**: Uses experiments or multimethod designs pairing economic (agency, incentive) and behavioral or relational lenses, e.g., showing formal controls crowd out value-based or social levers.
  - *now*: Uses multimethod designs (qualitative case plus experiment, field plus lab) or pairs economic and behavioral lenses, e.g., formal controls crowding out social levers.
  - *why*: A qualitative case combined with experiments marked the Organization Science side but carried too little weight.
- **EDITED**: Theorizes how internal organizational design—delegation, autonomy, internal resource or capital allocation, parent–unit linkages, interorganizational relationships—shapes units' behavior, often across multiple levels.
  - *now*: Theorizes how internal design or governance actors—delegation, decision structure, founders, boards, stakeholders, investors—shape what happens inside organizations, often across levels.
  - *why*: Organization Science articles stressed mechanisms inside the organization, such as investor–founder interaction or stakeholder salience, rather than market-level outcomes.
