# Changelog v6 (revision of v4)

**REJECTED** at iteration 6: val accuracy 87.5% (vs SMJ 97.6%, vs MgmtSci 76.3%); best so far 88.8%, threshold 87.8%. Revision of v4.

Train pool: v4 judged 39 train pairs, accuracy 97.4% (vs SMJ 100.0%, vs MgmtSci 95.0%). Reflection saw 1 misjudged, 8 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes, and how organizations trigger, avoid or resolve the resulting evaluation penalties.
  - *now*: Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes, and how actors avoid or resolve the resulting penalties, or turn them into advantages.
  - *why*: Organization Science winners showed how actors escape or reverse a penalty; the rivals only documented a penalty and when it gets worse.
- **EDITED**: Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning and search, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status dynamics, entrepreneurial creation, inequality inside organizations.
  - *now*: Advances core organization-theory conversations: learning and search, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status, entrepreneurship and careers, inequality inside organizations.
  - *why*: Studies of career transitions into entrepreneurship, shaped by the employment context, were Organization Science articles but were undervalued.
- **EDITED**: Uses experiments, multimethod designs (e.g., qualitative case plus experiment) or field data to isolate mechanisms, including how formal controls and social levers interact.
  - *now*: Pairs designs to test a mechanism: experiments, qualitative fieldwork plus experiments, or archival data plus a policy-capturing or vignette study.
  - *why*: Organization Science winners combined archival data with policy-capturing studies, or qualitative work with experiments; plain field data did not set them apart.
- **EDITED**: Accepts formal or simulation models deriving how actors' beliefs, learning or organizational policies produce unintended consequences, such as equity practices backfiring.
  - *now*: Theorizes paradoxes and unintended or second-order effects: practices undermining their own goals or spilling onto other stakeholders; formal or simulation models of this welcome.
  - *why*: Paradoxes that undermine themselves and spillovers onto other stakeholders marked Organization Science articles across all methods, not just formal models.
- **EDITED**: Welcomes inductive qualitative studies building new constructs or process models of identity tensions, discourse, inequality or unintended consequences of organizational practices.
  - *now*: Welcomes inductive qualitative studies showing how organizational practices or role hierarchies produce identity tensions, inequality or unintended consequences, even against large-sample rivals.
  - *why*: The board favored a large-sample gender-gap study over an interview study of intraoccupational segregation, which Organization Science published.
- **EDITED**: Economics-style causal identification (instruments, exogenous legal or policy shocks) foregrounded, or program and law evaluation closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions.
  - *now*: Economics-style identification foregrounded (instruments, policy shocks, randomized assignment yielding 'robust econometric patterns'), or closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions.
  - *why*: An econometric study built on randomized worker assignment belonged elsewhere but was not flagged.
- **EDITED**: Game-theoretic or optimal-design models of strategic interaction, incentives or contracting; Organization Science formal models instead derive learning, belief or adaptation dynamics.
  - *now*: Game-theoretic, optimal-design or two-sided matching/assortative-sorting frameworks of incentives, contracting or partner selection; Organization Science models instead derive learning, belief or adaptation dynamics.
  - *why*: Economic matching frameworks signal another journal and were only weakly caught.
- **EDITED**: Explains pay, productivity or wage gaps via individual skills, credentials or labor-market screening, without the employing organization's status, structure, power or process.
  - *now*: Explains pay, productivity or turnover purely via individual skills, sorting or screening, without how jobs, organizations or institutions shape careers and choices.
  - *why*: The old rule wrongly penalized Organization Science papers where job match or institutional constraints shape human capital and career choices.
- **EDITED**: Headlines a precise effect size, or merely the existence of a bias, penalty or gap, as the core result rather than a theorized organizational process.
  - *now*: Core result is documenting, sizing or locating (e.g., supply versus demand side) a bias, gap or penalty, or a single cognitive bias, not a theorized organizational process.
  - *why*: Effect sizes show up in Organization Science papers too; the true signal is studies that decompose a gap or isolate a single cognitive bias.
