## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how actors interpret signals, feedback, claims or categories: audiences evaluating organizations, or organizations reading market feedback to choose positions.
2. Posits countervailing effects or trade-offs across stages, life cycles or conditions, e.g., a practice or technology shifting costs from one stage to another.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, identity, authenticity, trust, attention, symbolic compliance or stakeholder salience, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual beliefs, cognition or relationships to network structure, hierarchy or group processes, showing how position and lay beliefs about the social order shape behavior.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning and search, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status dynamics, entrepreneurial creation, inequality inside organizations.
6. Reconciles conflicting findings, puzzles or competing camps through contingencies built from named theoretical mechanisms, not convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
7. Uses experiments, multimethod designs or field data to isolate mechanisms, including how formal controls and social levers interact.
8. Accepts formal, simulation or agency models when they derive how actors' divergent beliefs, learning, search or organizational structure produce outcomes or unintended consequences.
9. Theorizes organization design: structure, departmentalization, division of labor, coordination and communication costs, and how structures fit or adapt to changing tasks.
10. Welcomes inductive qualitative or purely conceptual work building new constructs or process models, e.g., identity tensions, climates, unintended consequences of practices.
11. Theorizes temporal or reciprocal dynamics, e.g., how status, evaluation and performance feed back, reinforce or decouple over repeated rounds.
12. Accepts studies of sourcing, firm boundaries or interorganizational arrangements when social context such as generalized trust, norms or relationships drives the choice.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Program or law evaluation headlining the policy's effect and a policy verdict, with economic or post-hoc mechanisms; quasi-experiments testing named social mechanisms are fine.
2. Industrial-organization models of market competition, licensing, pricing or equilibrium tested on industry panels, rather than models of organizational actors' beliefs, learning or structure.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset, typology, literature review or research-agenda editorial, rather than a theoretical mechanism.
4. Framed for strategy audiences: competitive advantage, capability microfoundations, internal capital allocation, diversification, executive decision cognition, with thin social or organizational mechanism.
5. Explanation hinges on cross-national culture, legal origins, or one country's distinctive historical regime or campaign, rather than a general organizational mechanism.
6. Quantitative pattern-finding whose mechanisms are guessed after the fact; this does not apply to inductive qualitative theory-building.
7. Explains pay, productivity or wage gaps via individual skills, credentials or labor-market screening, without the employing organization's status, structure, power or process.
8. Headlines a precise effect size, or merely the existence of a bias, penalty or backfire, as the core result rather than a theorized organizational process.
