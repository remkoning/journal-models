## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, language, categories or actor attributes, and how sender characteristics trigger, avoid or resolve evaluation penalties.
2. Posits countervailing or self-limiting effects: the same feature that yields an advantage at one stage or condition creates a disadvantage at another.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, identity, authenticity, trust, reciprocity, reputation or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual beliefs, cognition or relationships to network structure, formal or informal hierarchy, or group processes such as integration, helping and mobility.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning and search, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status dynamics, entrepreneurial creation, inequality inside organizations.
6. Reconciles conflicting findings, competing camps or competing views through contingencies built from named theoretical mechanisms, not convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
7. Combines economic levers (ownership, incentives, pay, formal controls) with relational or behavioral lenses, showing when formal practices enable or crowd out social mechanisms.
8. Accepts formal or simulation models, including agency models, when driven by actors' divergent beliefs, learning or policies that produce unintended consequences.
9. Welcomes inductive qualitative studies building new constructs or process models of identity tensions, discourse, inequality or unintended consequences of organizational practices.
10. Welcomes purely conceptual papers developing new organization-level constructs, such as climates or collective relational resources, with their antecedents and consequences.
11. Theorizes temporal or reciprocal dynamics, e.g., how status, evaluation and performance feed back, reinforce or decouple over repeated rounds.
12. Theorizes how internal organizational design—delegation, autonomy, internal resource or capital allocation, parent–unit linkages, interorganizational relationships—shapes units' behavior, often across multiple levels.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Policy-evaluation framing: one law, shock or program's causal effect identified and closed with prescriptions, without contrasting theorized organizational mechanisms; mechanism-testing quasi-experiments are fine.
2. Formal models built on financing, capital-structure, contracting or market frictions tied to strategy or profit payoffs, rather than actors' beliefs, learning or adaptation.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset, typology, literature review or research-agenda editorial, rather than a theoretical mechanism.
4. Strategy framing: competitive advantage, firm performance or stock-market returns of strategic moves (acquisitions, brokerage positions), capability microfoundations, strategic human capital, with thin organizational mechanism.
5. Explanation hinges on cross-national cultural values, legal origins or a country's historical regime; local social categories illustrating general mechanisms like homophily are fine.
6. Quantitative pattern-finding with no mechanism theorized beforehand; post hoc checks of mechanisms that were hypothesized up front are fine.
7. Explains careers, pay, productivity or gaps via individual skills, credentials or labor-market screening, without the organization's relationships, status, structure, power or process.
8. Headlines a precise effect size, or merely the existence of a bias, penalty, backfire or gap, as the core result rather than a theorized organizational process.
