## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes, and how actors avoid or resolve the resulting penalties, or turn them into advantages.
2. Posits countervailing effects or trade-offs across stages, life cycles or conditions, e.g., a practice or technology shifting costs from one stage to another.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, identity, authenticity, trust, reciprocity, reputation or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual beliefs, cognition or relationships to network structure, hierarchy or group processes, showing how position and lay beliefs about the social order shape behavior.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: learning and search, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status, entrepreneurship and careers, inequality inside organizations.
6. Reconciles conflicting findings, competing camps or competing views through contingencies built from named theoretical mechanisms, not convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
7. Pairs designs to test a mechanism: experiments, qualitative fieldwork plus experiments, or archival data plus a policy-capturing or vignette study.
8. Theorizes paradoxes and unintended or second-order effects: practices undermining their own goals or spilling onto other stakeholders; formal or simulation models of this welcome.
9. Welcomes inductive qualitative studies showing how organizational practices or role hierarchies produce identity tensions, inequality or unintended consequences, even against large-sample rivals.
10. Welcomes purely conceptual papers developing new organization-level constructs, such as climates or collective relational resources, with their antecedents and consequences.
11. Theorizes temporal or reciprocal dynamics, e.g., how status, evaluation and performance feed back, reinforce or decouple over repeated rounds.
12. Accepts studies of sourcing, firm boundaries or interorganizational arrangements when social context such as generalized trust, norms or relationships drives the choice.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Economics-style identification foregrounded (instruments, policy shocks, randomized assignment yielding 'robust econometric patterns'), or closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions.
2. Game-theoretic, optimal-design or two-sided matching/assortative-sorting frameworks of incentives, contracting or partner selection; Organization Science models instead derive learning, belief or adaptation dynamics.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset, typology, literature review or research-agenda editorial, rather than a theoretical mechanism.
4. Framed for strategy audiences: competitive advantage, capability microfoundations, strategic human capital, scale or scope advantages, with thin sociological or behavioral mechanism.
5. Explanation hinges on cross-national culture, legal origins, or one country's distinctive historical regime or campaign, rather than a general organizational mechanism.
6. Quantitative pattern-finding whose mechanisms are guessed after the fact; this does not apply to inductive qualitative theory-building.
7. Explains pay, productivity or turnover purely via individual skills, sorting or screening, without how jobs, organizations or institutions shape careers and choices.
8. Core result is documenting, sizing or locating (e.g., supply versus demand side) a bias, gap or penalty, or a single cognitive bias, not a theorized organizational process.
