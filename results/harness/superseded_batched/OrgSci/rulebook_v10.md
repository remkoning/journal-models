## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, language, categories or actor attributes, and how sender characteristics trigger, avoid or resolve evaluation penalties.
2. Posits countervailing or self-limiting effects, where an advantage in one stage or condition becomes a liability in another, and shows how organizations navigate that tension.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, identity, authenticity, trust, reciprocity, reputation or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual beliefs, cognition or relationships to network structure, formal or informal hierarchy, or group processes such as integration, helping and mobility.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning, search and its cognitive microfoundations, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status dynamics, entrepreneurship, intra-organizational inequality.
6. Challenges a dominant explanation of an established regularity with an alternative mechanism, or reconciles competing camps through contingencies built from named theoretical mechanisms.
7. Combines economic levers (ownership, incentives, pay, formal controls) with relational or behavioral lenses, showing when formal practices enable or crowd out social mechanisms.
8. Accepts formal or simulation models of organizational learning, search or adaptation in which actors' divergent beliefs or policies produce emergent, unintended consequences.
9. Welcomes inductive qualitative or mixed-method studies building new constructs or process models of identity, status, discourse, inequality or unintended consequences of practices.
10. Welcomes conceptual papers developing new organization-level constructs, and dissertation summaries integrating several essays and theories around one organizational phenomenon.
11. Theorizes temporal or reciprocal dynamics, e.g., how status, evaluation, performance or people's movement between organizations feed back, reinforce, decay or decouple over time.
12. Theorizes internal design and firm boundaries—delegation, capital allocation, make-or-buy, theory-of-the-firm debates, interorganizational arrangements—shaping unit behavior or industry structure.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Policy-evaluation framing: one law, shock or program's causal effect identified and closed with prescriptions, without contrasting theorized organizational mechanisms; mechanism-testing quasi-experiments are fine.
2. Formal models built on financing, capital-structure, contracting, real options or market frictions tied to strategy or profit payoffs, or on executive biases predicting firm risk.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset, typology, literature review or research agenda; this is a stronger warning than an effect-size headline.
4. Strategy framing: competitive advantage, firm performance, growth-option value or investor reactions and abnormal returns to strategic or political moves, with thin organizational mechanism.
5. Explanation hinges on cross-national cultural values, legal origins or a country's historical regime; national contexts explained through status, identity or homophily mechanisms are fine.
6. Quantitative pattern-finding with no mechanism theorized beforehand; post hoc checks of mechanisms that were hypothesized up front are fine.
7. Explains careers, pay, productivity or gaps via individual skills, credentials or labor-market screening, without the organization's relationships, status, structure, power or process.
8. Headlines precise percentages or merely the existence of a bias, penalty or gap as the core result, rather than a theorized organizational or status process.
