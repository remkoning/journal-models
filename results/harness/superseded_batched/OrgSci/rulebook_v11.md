## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, language, categories or actor attributes, and how sender characteristics trigger, avoid or resolve evaluation penalties.
2. Posits countervailing or self-limiting effects: the same feature that yields an advantage at one stage or condition creates a disadvantage at another.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, identity, authenticity, trust, reciprocity, reputation or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual beliefs, cognition or relationships to network structure, formal or informal hierarchy, or group processes such as integration, helping and mobility.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: learning, search and exploration (e.g., overcoming local search via hires or networks), legitimacy, categories, status, entrepreneurship, intra-organizational inequality.
6. Reconciles conflicting findings, competing camps or competing views through contingencies built from named theoretical mechanisms, not convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
7. Combines economic levers (incentives, monitoring, pay, formal controls) with relational or values-based lenses, showing when formal controls enable, crowd out or negate social mechanisms.
8. Accepts formal or simulation models of boundedly rational actors—fallible judgment, learning, search, aggregating views—where organizational structures or policies yield unintended consequences.
9. Welcomes inductive qualitative or purely conceptual work building new constructs, process models, or organization-level constructs (e.g., climates, collective resources) with antecedents and consequences.
10. Theorizes temporal or reciprocal dynamics, e.g., how status, evaluation and performance feed back, reinforce or decouple over repeated rounds.
11. Theorizes how internal organizational design—delegation, autonomy, internal resource or capital allocation, parent–unit linkages, interorganizational relationships—shapes units' behavior, often across multiple levels.
12. Uses field or lab experiments to test how a managerial practice or organizational lever shapes coordination, collaboration or conduct, revealing theorized trade-offs.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Policy-evaluation framing: one law, shock or program's causal effect identified and closed with prescriptions, without contrasting theorized organizational mechanisms; mechanism-testing quasi-experiments are fine.
2. Contract-economics or mechanism-design logic—self-enforcing agreements, discounted relationship value, financing, optimal principal-agent processes solved for optimum—rather than actors' beliefs, learning or social relations.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset, typology, literature review or research-agenda editorial, rather than a theoretical mechanism.
4. Strategy framing: competitive advantage, firm performance or stock-market returns of strategic moves (acquisitions, brokerage positions), capability microfoundations, strategic human capital, with thin organizational mechanism.
5. Explanation hinges on national legal origins or a country's historical regime; regional social context (e.g., generalized trust) or local categories illustrating general mechanisms are fine.
6. Quantitative pattern-finding with no mechanism theorized beforehand; post hoc checks of mechanisms that were hypothesized up front are fine.
7. Explains careers, pay, productivity or gaps via individual skills, skill development, credentials or labor-market screening, without the organization's relationships, status, structure, power or process.
8. Headlines precise effect sizes or percentage gaps, or merely the existence of a bias, penalty, backfire or gap, rather than a theorized organizational process.
