# Management Science rulebook v4

**ACCEPTED** at iteration 4: val accuracy 92.5% (vs OrgSci 84.2%, vs SMJ 100.0%); best so far 92.5%, threshold 91.5%. Revision of v1.

## What Management Science values
1. Formal or economic frameworks (game-theoretic, principal-agent, matching, search, market equilibrium) that derive clear results about incentives, contracts, sorting, or welfare.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, contractibility, credibility, market failure, efficiency, collusion, or the costs and benefits of adoption.
3. Archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, sudden events, unexpected deaths, strict cutoffs, instruments) to identify causal effects.
4. Precise measurement of an important phenomenon, such as how widespread, fast, or large it is, even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity or policy.
5. Concrete quantified findings (percentages, effect sizes, profit or time gaps) reported directly in the abstract.
6. Controlled lab or field experiments, especially when combined with computational or formal modeling to separate mechanisms.
7. Comparing decision structures (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized, search strategies) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
8. Outcomes that can be measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, profits, productivity, sourcing, market share) rather than identities or perceptions.
9. Separating competing explanations or sources, such as supply vs. demand or team vs. individual effects, to locate where an effect really comes from.
10. A model or analysis that explains empirical patterns and draws out unexpected implications for value creation or policy.
11. Testing whether a common management practice or policy works, including its unintended consequences and why standard remedies fail.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or a single longitudinal case study that builds a process model or new theory.
2. Methodological commentary or recommendations aimed at researchers in one field rather than a substantive finding.
3. Coining a new concept, paradox, or named hypothesis as the headline contribution rather than a model result or a quantified finding.
4. Abstracts mostly made of 'we argue/theorize/hypothesize' statements with no results or magnitudes reported.
5. Framed mainly as extending a named management theory or literature, with neither a formal model nor a causal design to anchor it.
6. Main focus on subjective experience (identity, status, felt fit, stress, wellbeing) as the outcome or headline mechanism rather than behaviour or incentives.
7. Theorizing propositions after the fact from observational patterns ('we propose a self-limiting nature') instead of testing or modeling them.
8. Correlational panel results ('is associated with') stacked with moderators or moderated mediation, with no exogenous variation addressing endogeneity.
