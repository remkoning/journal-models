## What Management Science values
1. Formal models (game-theoretic, principal-agent, dynamic, market equilibrium) that derive clear results about incentives, contracts, or welfare.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, contractibility, credibility, market failure, efficiency, collusion, or the costs and benefits of adoption.
3. Large-scale archival, administrative, platform, census, or proprietary transaction data analysed with careful identification or natural experiments.
4. Explicit causal identification: random or quasi-random assignment, exogenous shocks (unexpected deaths, law or budget changes), instruments, or difference-in-differences.
5. Precise measurement of an important phenomenon, such as how widespread, fast, or large it is, even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity or policy.
6. Concrete quantified findings (percentages, effect sizes, profit or time gaps) reported directly in the abstract.
7. Randomized field experiments in real organizations with real outcomes; lab experiments mainly when paired with formal or computational modeling.
8. Comparing decision structures (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
9. Outcomes that can be measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, hiring, turnover, capital raised, revenue, productivity) rather than identities or perceptions.
10. Tracing an effect to one channel by ruling out rival explanations (supply vs. demand, team vs. individual, learning vs. signaling).
11. Directly testing a well-known economic theory or common practitioner claim with market data, including null or reversed results.
12. A model or analysis that explains empirical patterns and draws out unexpected implications for value creation or policy.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or a single longitudinal case study that builds a process model or new theory.
2. Methodological commentary or recommendations aimed at researchers in one field rather than a substantive finding.
3. A newly coined concept or label as the headline contribution, unsupported by causal identification, a formal model, or quantified large-scale evidence.
4. Abstracts mostly made of 'we argue/theorize/hypothesize' statements with no results or magnitudes reported.
5. Framed mainly as extending a named management theory, advancing extant research, or bridging disciplines rather than answering a substantive question.
6. Main focus on subjective perceptions (status, identity, authenticity) tested via vignette or multi-study lab experiments without market or behavioural outcomes.
7. Theorizing propositions after the fact from observational patterns ('we propose a self-limiting nature') instead of testing or modeling them.
8. Headline results are contingency or moderation interactions (effect depends on stage, hierarchy, orientation) from observational correlations with no identification strategy.
