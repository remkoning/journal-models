## What Management Science values
1. Formal or economic models (game theory, principal-agent, matching, search, equilibrium) with characterized optimal choices; pure analytical models count fully even without data.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, signaling, cheap talk, information asymmetry, credibility, contractibility, market failure, efficiency, or costs and benefits of adoption.
3. Archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, sudden events, cutoffs, instruments) to identify causal effects.
4. Precise, comprehensive measurement of an important phenomenon (prevalence, speed, size), even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity, governance, or policy.
5. Concrete quantified findings in the abstract: percentages, effect sizes, per-unit responses, share of a gap closed, profit or time differences.
6. Randomized lab, field, or platform experiments with a sharp question and a headline behavioural or economic magnitude, ideally preregistered.
7. New measures or computational tools (text, machine-learning linkage, clustering) used to deliver substantive findings about a phenomenon, not merely to critique existing measures.
8. Comparing decision structures or problem-solving processes (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized, search order or strategy) through effects on choices, innovations, or performance.
9. Outcomes measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, applications, salary demands, profits, productivity, innovation output) rather than identities or perceptions.
10. Separating competing explanations or decomposing an effect, such as supply vs. demand or which observations drive it, to locate where it comes from.
11. Testing whether a common management practice or policy works, including unintended consequences, firm workarounds, and why standard remedies fail.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or single in-depth case studies that build a process model or new theory, even when paired with small experiments.
2. Methodological commentary aimed at researchers, including critiques of an existing measure's error with proposed fixes, when no new substantive finding results.
3. Coining a new concept, paradox, or named hypothesis as the headline contribution, even when supported by a formal model, field data, or experiments.
4. Abstracts dominated by 'we argue/theorize/propose' statements without reported results or magnitudes, or propositions inferred post hoc from observed patterns.
5. Contributions aimed at named sociological or organizational literatures (categories, legitimacy, embeddedness, social comparison, optimal distinctiveness) without a formal model or causal design.
6. Subjective experience (identity, status, satisfaction, trust, stress, mental health, wellbeing) as outcome or headline mechanism, even inside a randomized experiment.
7. Correlational panel results ('is associated with') stacked with moderators or moderated mediation, with no exogenous variation addressing endogeneity.
8. Mechanism claims that rest on speculative heterogeneity splits ('hint at', 'suggest') rather than direct tests or quantified economic responses.
