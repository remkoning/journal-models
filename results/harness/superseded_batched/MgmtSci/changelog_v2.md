# Changelog v2 (revision of v1)

**REJECTED** at iteration 2: val accuracy 88.8% (vs OrgSci 78.9%, vs SMJ 97.6%); best so far 92.5%, threshold 91.5%. Revision of v1.

Train pool: v1 judged 40 train pairs, accuracy 96.2% (vs OrgSci 92.5%, vs SMJ 100.0%). Reflection saw 2 misjudged, 7 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **ADDED**: Explicit causal identification: random or quasi-random assignment, exogenous shocks (unexpected deaths, law or budget changes), instruments, or difference-in-differences.
  - *why*: Management Science winners repeatedly named a concrete identification source while their counterparts only correlated or theorized.
- **EDITED**: Controlled lab or field experiments, especially when combined with computational or formal modeling to separate mechanisms.
  - *now*: Randomized field experiments in real organizations with real outcomes; lab experiments mainly when paired with formal or computational modeling.
  - *why*: The board over-rewarded lab or real-effort experiments, which lost to field experiments or archival data with identification.
- **EDITED**: Outcomes that can be measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, hiring, profits, productivity, sourcing, market share) rather than identities or perceptions.
  - *now*: Outcomes that can be measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, hiring, turnover, capital raised, revenue, productivity) rather than identities or perceptions.
  - *why*: Widened the examples to cover the outcomes typical of the winning abstracts.
- **EDITED**: Separating competing explanations or sources, such as supply vs. demand or aggregation vs. context effects, to locate where an effect really comes from.
  - *now*: Tracing an effect to one channel by ruling out rival explanations (supply vs. demand, team vs. individual, learning vs. signaling).
  - *why*: Winners decomposed effects into channels and excluded alternatives; sharpened the rule so this is easier to spot.
- **ADDED**: Directly testing a well-known economic theory or common practitioner claim with market data, including null or reversed results.
  - *why*: A winner tested an established economic theory against market outcomes, while its counterpart offered a contingency-based network story.
- **EDITED**: Coining a new concept or label as the headline contribution rather than a model result or a quantified finding.
  - *now*: A newly coined concept or label as the headline contribution, unsupported by causal identification, a formal model, or quantified large-scale evidence.
  - *why*: Both misjudged winners introduced a construct but backed it with large data and identification; the rule penalised them unfairly.
- **EDITED**: Framed mainly as extending a named management theory (resource dependence, behavioral theory of the firm, identity) or contributing to a literature.
  - *now*: Framed mainly as extending a named management theory, advancing extant research, or bridging disciplines rather than answering a substantive question.
  - *why*: Losing abstracts framed their contribution as advancing research or giving disciplines a meeting ground.
- **EDITED**: Main focus on how people subjectively experience status, gender, or identity, measured through felt appropriateness or beliefs.
  - *now*: Main focus on subjective perceptions (status, identity, authenticity) tested via vignette or multi-study lab experiments without market or behavioural outcomes.
  - *why*: Losers tested perception hypotheses with lab or vignette studies, while winners on similar topics used observed market behaviour.
- **ADDED**: Headline results are contingency or moderation interactions (effect depends on stage, hierarchy, orientation) from observational correlations with no identification strategy.
  - *why*: Several losing abstracts centred on moderation patterns in observational data without any causal design.
