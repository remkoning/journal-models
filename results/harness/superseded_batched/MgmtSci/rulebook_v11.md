## What Management Science values
1. Formal economic models (game theory, contracting, matching, search, equilibrium) with comparative statics; pure analytical models of competition, vertical relations, entry or regulation count without data.
2. Economic mechanisms: incentives, signaling, cheap talk, information asymmetry, screening or employer learning, credibility, contractibility, foreclosure, market failure, or adoption costs and benefits.
3. Archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, cutoffs, instruments, naturally random assignment) to identify causal effects.
4. Precise, comprehensive measurement of an important phenomenon (prevalence, speed, size), even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity, governance, or policy.
5. Concrete quantified findings in the abstract: percentages, effect sizes, per-unit responses, monetized impacts, share of a gap closed, profit or time differences.
6. Randomized lab, field, or platform experiments, ideally preregistered; these beat quasi-experiments whose shock reaches treatment only through remote, multi-step causal chains.
7. New measures or computational tools (text, network, validated instruments) as the headline contribution, applied to data; often outrank a single causal estimate.
8. Comparing decision structures (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized, search strategies) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
9. Outcomes measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, applications, scores, earnings, profits, offshoring), even when the proposed mechanism is psychological.
10. Separating competing explanations or decomposing an effect, such as supply vs. demand or which observations drive it, to locate where it comes from.
11. Testing whether a common management practice or policy works, including unintended consequences, firm workarounds, and why standard remedies fail.
12. Absent models or causal designs on both sides, prefer the large-sample quantitative test over qualitative or purely conceptual work, whatever its theoretical framing.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or single in-depth case studies that build a process model or new theory, even when paired with small experiments.
2. Methodological commentary or best-practice advice to researchers; this does not apply to new formal or computational methods actually applied to data.
3. Coining a new concept, paradox, or labelled construct as the headline, especially inside a formal model listing many propositions but no data.
4. Abstracts dominated by 'we argue/theorize/propose' statements without reported results or magnitudes, or propositions inferred post hoc from observed patterns.
5. Framing around sociological literatures (categories, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, institutional contextualization) rather than economic mechanisms, when not paired with causal identification.
6. Subjective experience (identity, status, felt fit, trust, wellbeing) as the measured outcome; psychological mechanisms are acceptable when outcomes are behaviour under random assignment.
7. Correlational panel results ('is associated with') stacked with moderators or moderated mediation, with no exogenous variation addressing endogeneity.
8. Mechanism claims that rest on speculative heterogeneity splits ('hint at', 'suggest') rather than direct tests or quantified economic responses.
