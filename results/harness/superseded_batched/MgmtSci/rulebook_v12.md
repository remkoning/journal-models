# Management Science rulebook v12

**ACCEPTED** at iteration 12: val accuracy 92.5% (vs OrgSci 84.2%, vs SMJ 100.0%); best so far 92.5%, threshold 91.5%. Revision of v7.

## What Management Science values
1. Formal or economic models (game theory, principal-agent, matching, search, equilibrium) with clear results; pure analytical models with optimal choices count, and data tests add strength.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, signaling, cheap talk, information asymmetry, credibility, contractibility, market failure, efficiency, or costs and benefits of adoption.
3. Exogenous variation (policy shocks, sudden events, cutoffs, instruments) in archival or proprietary data counts as causal design even when results are worded 'associated with'.
4. Precise, comprehensive measurement of an important phenomenon (prevalence, speed, size), even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity, governance, or policy.
5. Concrete quantified findings in the abstract: percentages, effect sizes, per-unit responses, share of a gap closed, profit or time differences.
6. Randomized lab, field, or platform experiments on behaviour, ideally preregistered, which count as stronger evidence than quasi-experiments with long causal chains.
7. New quantitative measures or computational tools (text-based, machine-learning linking, validated instruments) that make a hard-to-observe construct measurable at scale and are applied to data.
8. Comparing decision structures or processes (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized, search order or strategy) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
9. Outcomes measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, applications, ratings, profits, productivity, offshoring, market share) rather than identities or perceptions.
10. Separating competing explanations or decomposing an effect, such as supply vs. demand or which observations drive it, to locate where it comes from.
11. Testing whether a common management practice or policy works, including unintended consequences, firm workarounds, and why standard remedies fail.
12. Empirical work organized by an established economic framework (two-sided matching, assortative sorting, search) beats framework-free relationship mapping, even when results are only suggestive.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or single in-depth case studies that build a process model or new theory, even when paired with small experiments.
2. Methodological commentary for researchers, including critiques of an existing measure's error with proposed fixes; does not apply to new methods answering a substantive question.
3. Coining a new concept, paradox, or labelled hypothesis as the headline contribution, even when tested with field data, an experiment, or a formal model.
4. Abstracts dominated by 'we argue/theorize/posit' chains, with data confirming only one piece or reported without results or magnitudes.
5. Contributions aimed at named literatures (categories, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, embeddedness, behavioral theory of the firm) without a formal model or causal design.
6. Subjective experience (identity, status, job satisfaction, perceived value, trust, stress, mental health) as the outcome or headline mechanism, rather than behaviour or incentives.
7. Correlational panel results ('is associated with') stacked with moderators or moderated mediation, with no exogenous variation addressing endogeneity.
8. Mechanism claims that rest on speculative heterogeneity splits ('hint at', 'suggest') rather than direct tests or quantified economic responses.
