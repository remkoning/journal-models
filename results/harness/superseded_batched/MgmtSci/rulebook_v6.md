## What Management Science values
1. Formal or economic frameworks (game-theoretic, principal-agent, matching, search, market equilibrium) that derive clear results about incentives, contracts, sorting, or welfare.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, contractibility, credibility, market failure, efficiency, collusion, or the costs and benefits of adoption.
3. Large archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, sudden deaths, random or quasi-random assignment, cutoffs, instruments).
4. Precise measurement of an important phenomenon, such as how widespread, fast, or large it is, even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity or policy.
5. Concrete quantified findings (percentages, effect sizes, profit or time gaps) reported directly in the abstract.
6. Field experiments or quasi-random assignment inside real organizations; lab experiments mainly when incentivized and paired with formal or computational modeling.
7. Comparing decision structures (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized, search strategies) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
8. Outcomes that can be measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, capital raised, revenues, productivity, violations) rather than identities or perceptions.
9. Separating competing explanations or sources (supply vs. demand, team vs. individual, detection vs. behavior) via decomposition, fixed effects, or targeted tests.
10. A model or analysis that explains empirical patterns and draws out unexpected implications for value creation or policy.
11. Testing whether a common management practice or policy works, including its unintended consequences and why standard remedies fail.
12. Biases or information frictions in evaluators, consumers, or monitors documented in real-world decisions and market data, not hypothetical scenarios.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or a single longitudinal case study that builds a process model or new theory.
2. Methodological commentary or recommendations aimed at researchers in one field rather than a substantive finding.
3. Coining a new concept, paradox, or named hypothesis as the headline contribution, when not backed by causal identification or large-scale behavioral evidence.
4. Abstracts mostly made of 'we argue/theorize/hypothesize' statements with no results or magnitudes reported.
5. Framed mainly as extending a named management theory (routines, path dependence, legitimacy) or bridging literatures, rather than resolving an economic question.
6. Main focus on subjective experience (identity, status, felt fit, authenticity, stress) as the outcome or headline mechanism rather than behaviour or incentives.
7. Headline result is 'it depends': effects contingent on stage, structure, or hierarchy via interactions in correlational data without exogenous variation.
8. Vignette or multi-study lab experiments measuring hypothetical perceptions or evaluations, rather than real choices with stakes in field settings.
