## What Management Science values
1. Analytic formal models (game-theoretic, principal-agent, matching, search, equilibrium) that characterize optimal policies or equilibria and derive clear results on incentives or welfare.
2. Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, information asymmetry, signaling or cheap talk, noncontractibility, self-enforcing contracts, credibility, market failure, collusion, or efficiency.
3. Archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, sudden events, unexpected deaths, strict cutoffs, instruments) to identify causal effects.
4. Precise measurement of an important phenomenon, such as how widespread, fast, or large it is, even if mostly descriptive, when it informs productivity or policy.
5. New measurement built for the question (original surveys, mystery shoppers, text-based measures validated experimentally) that makes a hard-to-observe variable testable.
6. Concrete quantified findings (percentages, effect sizes, profit or time gaps) reported directly in the abstract.
7. Randomized lab or field experiments testing an intervention or decision approach on real firms or workers, especially combined with formal modeling of mechanisms.
8. Comparing decision structures (individuals vs. groups, centralized vs. decentralized, search strategies) through their effects on choices, learning, or performance.
9. Outcomes measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, profits, sourcing, market share), even in psychology studies, rather than beliefs or perceptions.
10. Separating competing explanations or sources, such as supply vs. demand or team vs. individual effects, to locate where an effect really comes from.
11. A model guided by causal or descriptive empirical results that explains the patterns and draws unexpected implications for value creation, welfare, or policy.
12. Testing whether a common management practice or policy works, including its unintended consequences and why standard remedies fail.

## What Management Science does not value / red flags
1. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, or a single longitudinal case study that builds a process model or new theory.
2. Methodological commentary or recommendations aimed at researchers in one field rather than a substantive finding.
3. Headline is a newly coined construct, belief, or paradox whose effects are shown mainly through moderation in survey or vignette studies, not behavioural data.
4. Abstract reports only argued, theorized, postulated, or simulated implications ('we argue', 'the model suggests') without empirical tests or analytic results.
5. Framed as extending a named management theory, or invoking a theory only to motivate a hypothesis, with neither a formal model nor causal design.
6. Main outcome is subjective experience or beliefs (identity, status felt, fit, wellbeing, perceived value); perceptions as mediators of behaviour are acceptable.
7. Contribution framed as resolving an organizational tension or trade-off (novelty vs. value, tradition vs. innovation, collective vs. individual) rather than an economic mechanism.
8. Correlational results ('is associated with', 'suggestive evidence') stacked with moderators or moderated mediation, with no exogenous variation addressing endogeneity.
