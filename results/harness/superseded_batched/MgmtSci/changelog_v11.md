# Changelog v11 (revision of v7)

**REJECTED** at iteration 11: val accuracy 90.0% (vs OrgSci 84.2%, vs SMJ 95.2%); best so far 92.5%, threshold 91.5%. Revision of v7.

Train pool: v7 judged 40 train pairs, accuracy 100.0% (vs OrgSci 100.0%, vs SMJ 100.0%). Reflection saw 0 misjudged, 9 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: Formal or economic models (game theory, principal-agent, matching, search, equilibrium) with clear results, best when their predictions are then tested on large-sample data.
  - *now*: Formal economic models (game theory, contracting, matching, search, equilibrium) with comparative statics; pure analytical models of competition, vertical relations, entry or regulation count without data.
  - *why*: Pure analytical models of vertical relations and competition won against single-firm empirical work, but the board was only weakly confident.
- **EDITED**: Economics reasoning about mechanisms: incentives, signaling, cheap talk, information asymmetry, credibility, contractibility, market failure, efficiency, or costs and benefits of adoption.
  - *now*: Economic mechanisms: incentives, signaling, cheap talk, information asymmetry, screening or employer learning, credibility, contractibility, foreclosure, market failure, or adoption costs and benefits.
  - *why*: Winners framed labor-market questions as screening or employer-learning problems, while losers used institutional framings.
- **EDITED**: Archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, sudden events, cutoffs, instruments) to identify causal effects.
  - *now*: Archival, administrative, platform, or proprietary data with exogenous variation (policy shocks, cutoffs, instruments, naturally random assignment) to identify causal effects.
  - *why*: Random assignment that occurs naturally in real settings was underweighted as an identification strategy.
- **EDITED**: Concrete quantified findings in the abstract: percentages, effect sizes, per-unit responses, share of a gap closed, profit or time differences.
  - *now*: Concrete quantified findings in the abstract: percentages, effect sizes, per-unit responses, monetized impacts, share of a gap closed, profit or time differences.
  - *why*: Monetized effect sizes (earnings differences) marked the winners.
- **EDITED**: Randomized lab, field, or platform experiments on behaviour, ideally preregistered, which count as stronger evidence than quasi-experiments with long causal chains.
  - *now*: Randomized lab, field, or platform experiments, ideally preregistered; these beat quasi-experiments whose shock reaches treatment only through remote, multi-step causal chains.
  - *why*: A preregistered field experiment beat a remote-shock natural experiment, but the board was only weakly confident; this sharpens the comparison.
- **EDITED**: New quantitative measures or computational tools (text-based, clustering, validated instruments) that make a hard-to-observe construct measurable at scale and are applied to data.
  - *now*: New measures or computational tools (text, network, validated instruments) as the headline contribution, applied to data; often outrank a single causal estimate.
  - *why*: Papers whose headline was a new measure won over same-topic papers with clean quantified causal estimates.
- **EDITED**: Outcomes measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, applications, ratings, profits, productivity, offshoring, market share) rather than identities or perceptions.
  - *now*: Outcomes measured as behaviour or economic results (effort, exit, hiring, applications, scores, earnings, profits, offshoring), even when the proposed mechanism is psychological.
  - *why*: A winner had a performance outcome but a stress-based mechanism, and the old wording made the board penalize it.
- **ADDED**: Absent models or causal designs on both sides, prefer the large-sample quantitative test over qualitative or purely conceptual work, whatever its theoretical framing.
  - *why*: A sociologically framed large-sample archival study won against a qualitative case study, and the board was unsure.
- **EDITED**: Coining a new concept, paradox, or labelled construct as the headline contribution, even when it is wrapped in a formal model with many propositions.
  - *now*: Coining a new concept, paradox, or labelled construct as the headline, especially inside a formal model listing many propositions but no data.
  - *why*: A construct-driven theory model with no data lost to a large-sample study with instruments; the board was only weakly confident.
- **EDITED**: Contributions aimed at named literatures (categories, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, behavioral theory of the firm, strain theory) without a formal model or causal design.
  - *now*: Framing around sociological literatures (categories, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, institutional contextualization) rather than economic mechanisms, when not paired with causal identification.
  - *why*: Category and institutional framings lost to economic framings (cheap talk, employer learning) even when both sides used large samples or text methods.
- **EDITED**: Subjective experience (identity, status, felt fit, trust, stress, wellbeing) as the outcome or headline mechanism, rather than behaviour or incentives.
  - *now*: Subjective experience (identity, status, felt fit, trust, wellbeing) as the measured outcome; psychological mechanisms are acceptable when outcomes are behaviour under random assignment.
  - *why*: The old rule penalized a randomized performance study for proposing a stress mechanism.
