# Changelog v6 (revision of v3)

**REJECTED** at iteration 6: val accuracy 80.0% (vs SMJ 96.7%, vs MgmtSci 63.3%); best so far 88.3%, threshold 87.3%. Revision of v3.

Train pool: v3 judged 39 train pairs, accuracy 93.6% (vs SMJ 97.4%, vs MgmtSci 90.0%). Reflection saw 3 misjudged, 6 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes to form evaluations, emphasizing social meaning-making rather than testing economic demand or discrimination models.
  - *now*: Theorizes how audiences, including lenders and investors, infer unobservable qualities such as intent, risk or competence from claims, language, categories or seemingly unrelated actor attributes.
  - *why*: Organization Science (OS) winners included financial stakeholders inferring trustworthiness or risk from behavior or traits seen elsewhere, and audiences reading speech; the old rule left these cases at about a coin flip.
- **EDITED**: Posits countervailing effects, paradoxes or trade-offs, such as a benefit at one stage or condition and a penalty at another, and specifies contingencies.
  - *now*: Posits countervailing effects, paradoxes or trade-offs, and specifies contingencies using moderators derived from named mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, not convenient proxies.
  - *why*: Merged with the old moderator rule to free a slot; the content is unchanged.
- **DROPPED**: Builds moderators from named theoretical mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, rather than from convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
  - *why*: Folded into the countervailing-effects rule.
- **ADDED**: Traces how audience expectations feed back into organizational choices, e.g., claims or identities that constrain a producer's growth, scale, production or repositioning.
  - *why*: The OS paper on audience evaluation tied perceptions to firm-level organizing consequences; its rival stopped at evaluator perception alone.
- **EDITED**: Links individual cognition or behavior to organizational structures, formal arrangements or group processes, e.g., how contract terms or hierarchy create pressures that shape decisions.
  - *now*: Explains individual or team outcomes, including careers and entrepreneurship, through organizational structures: hierarchy, formal arrangements, firm size, internal opportunities, networks or social integration.
  - *why*: Merged with the intraorganizational-relationships rule and extended to employer features shaping careers; the OS career paper beat an individual self-learning model.
- **DROPPED**: Explains individual or team outcomes through intraorganizational relationships, networks, social integration or position in formal and informal hierarchies.
  - *why*: Folded into the merged organizational-structure rule.
- **EDITED**: Uses experiments, quasi-experiments or field data to separate mechanisms or reveal hidden trade-offs of a management practice, not only whether it works.
  - *now*: Uses experiments, quasi-experiments, policy-capturing or mixed methods to separate mechanisms or reveal hidden trade-offs of a practice, not only whether it works.
  - *why*: OS winners paired archival data with policy-capturing or qualitative-plus-experimental designs to confirm a mechanism.
- **ADDED**: Theorizes how inter-organizational collaborations or shared standards reshape firm boundaries, vertical industry structure, or the capabilities firms need, weighing competing mechanisms.
  - *why*: The OS paper on a collaborative arrangement was underrated because it reported an effect size; its organizational-boundary theory was not credited.
- **ADDED**: Examines workers' subjective experience of status, identity or fit across work contexts, often grounding hypotheses in qualitative data before testing them.
  - *why*: The OS paper on how workers experience status, built from qualitative data and then experiments, was nearly lost to a loss-aversion paper with percentage effects.
- **EDITED**: Economics-style effect estimation, such as percentage-point gaps or policy evaluation using exogenous variation, closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions rather than organizational mechanism.
  - *now*: Economics-style effect estimation or policy evaluation ending in efficiency prescriptions; effect sizes alone are fine when organizational mechanisms drive the argument.
  - *why*: The board penalized an OS paper just for reporting a percentage effect, although its argument rested on organizational mechanisms.
- **EDITED**: Principal-agent, mechanism-design or relational-contract models where a principal optimizes incentives, matching or challenges; self-enforcement or discounted-value logic suggests economics journals.
  - *now*: Principal-agent, mechanism-design, relational-contract or individual sorting-and-self-learning models, where optimizing agents or discounted-value logic drive results, suggest economics or finance journals.
  - *why*: The non-OS rival was a formal model of employees learning their own fitness and sorting into other jobs.
- **EDITED**: The main contribution is a new measure, dataset or descriptive typology rather than a theoretical mechanism about organizations or audiences.
  - *now*: Main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset or typology; such papers lose to same-topic papers advancing a substantive organizational mechanism, even effect-estimating ones.
  - *why*: A paper built around a new index beat an OS paper with substantive theory; the flag needed to apply even when the OS paper reports effects.
- **EDITED**: Explains career or productivity outcomes through individual skill or human-capital accumulation, without social, relational, cognitive or organizational process.
  - *now*: Explains career or productivity outcomes through individual skill or human-capital accumulation alone; human capital is fine as a moderator of social or organizational processes.
  - *why*: An OS paper using founder human capital to moderate how audiences read language was wrongly penalized.
