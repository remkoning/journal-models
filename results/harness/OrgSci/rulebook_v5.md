## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes meaning-making: how audiences read signals, categories or claims, or how organizations interpret ambiguous feedback cues to decide under uncertainty.
2. Posits countervailing effects, trade-offs or self-limiting advantages with contingencies, framed as resolving tensions between theories or competing mechanisms.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, categories, identity, authenticity, cognition, tokenism or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual cognition or behavior to organizational structures, formal arrangements or group processes, e.g., how contract terms or hierarchy create pressures that shape decisions.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning, exploration and exploitation, search, optimal distinctiveness, categories, entrepreneurial creation, status dynamics.
6. Uses experiments, quasi-experiments or field data to contrast named theoretical mechanisms (e.g., symbolic versus substantive) or reveal hidden trade-offs, not just whether something works.
7. Accepts formal or simulation models of organizational processes, such as search, learning, aggregation or governance amid divergent beliefs, deriving propositions about organizational actors, not market welfare.
8. Builds moderators from named theoretical mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, rather than from convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
9. Theorizes interplay between formal controls (monitoring, incentives, contracts) and informal or normative ones (values, trust, relationships), including substitution or crowding out.
10. Explains individual or team outcomes through intraorganizational relationships, networks, social integration or position in formal and informal hierarchies.
11. Values purely conceptual theory-building that explains how organizational or institutional fields are structured, persist and change, including around grand challenges.
12. Traces how external pressures shape attention inside firms and consequences for employees or stakeholders, rather than only financial or growth performance.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Single policy or law evaluated via difference-in-differences, reporting an effect (even a backfire) and a policy lesson, without theorizing contrasting organizational mechanisms.
2. Models of market competition, pricing, platforms or consumer welfare, or relational contracts with self-enforcement logic; agency models centered on divergent beliefs are acceptable.
3. Main contribution is a new measure, dataset or descriptive empirical typology; conceptual typologies that theorize how structures emerge and change are acceptable.
4. Framed for strategy audiences: competitive advantage, M&A, diversification, internal capital allocation, TMT composition and firm growth, with thin social or behavioral mechanism; also literature reviews.
5. Explains via cross-national law, culture or individual values of elites, closing with legal or policy prescriptions rather than theorizing organizing processes.
6. Pattern-finding with mechanisms guessed post hoc; abductive theorizing that turns findings into a general theoretical proposition about organizing is acceptable.
7. Explains career or productivity outcomes through individual skill or human-capital accumulation, without social, relational, cognitive or organizational process.
8. Tests a classic economic theory, such as taste-based discrimination, network effects or foreclosure, against market outcomes like prices, revenues or sales.
