## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes, and how those evaluations constrain or enable what organizations can do.
2. Posits countervailing effects, paradoxes or trade-offs, such as a benefit at one stage or condition and a penalty at another, and specifies contingencies.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, identity, authenticity, trust, reciprocity, reputation or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links individual cognition or perceptions to organizational structures, governance or group processes, explaining how individual reactions aggregate into collective decisions or outcomes.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning, legitimacy, optimal distinctiveness, categories, status dynamics, entrepreneurial creation, inequality inside organizations.
6. Frames the contribution as resolving a tension between prevailing theory and emerging evidence, or between competing mechanisms, not just estimating an effect.
7. Uses quasi-experiments, experiments or field data to tell theoretical mechanisms apart (e.g., symbolic versus substantive), not only to test whether an intervention works.
8. Accepts formal, computational or simulation models that derive propositions about actors' beliefs, misalignment, learning or organizational processes.
9. Builds moderators from named theoretical mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, rather than from convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
10. Welcomes inductive qualitative studies that build new constructs showing how organizational practices produce identity tensions, inequality or unintended consequences.
11. Theorizes temporal or reciprocal dynamics, e.g., how status, evaluation and performance feed back, reinforce or decouple over repeated rounds.
12. Accepts organizational-economics arguments about interorganizational arrangements, firm boundaries or vertical structure when they explain organizational form and capabilities.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Economics-style evaluation of a program, law or practice using exogenous variation, closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions instead of organizational theory.
2. Formal incentive or contracting models centered on leverage, wages or effort efficiency, backed by stylized correlations, suggest economics or finance journals.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset or typology, even when pitched as enabling future theory, rather than a theoretical mechanism.
4. Framed around competitive advantage, capability deployment, scale or scope advantages, or industry emergence for strategy audiences, with thin sociological or behavioral mechanism.
5. Explanation hinges on cross-national culture, legal origins, or one country's distinctive regime or campaign, rather than a general organizational mechanism such as legitimacy.
6. Quantitative pattern-finding whose mechanisms are guessed after the fact; this does not apply to inductive qualitative theory-building.
7. Explains outcomes through individual skill or human-capital advantages from learning-by-doing, without social, cognitive or organizational process.
8. Applies decision biases or effect estimates to online platform or contest data, headlining precise percentages or design prescriptions rather than social mechanisms.
