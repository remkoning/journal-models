## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes to form evaluations, emphasizing social meaning-making rather than testing economic demand or discrimination models.
2. Posits countervailing effects, paradoxes or trade-offs, such as a benefit at one stage or condition and a penalty at another, and specifies contingencies.
3. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, categories, identity, authenticity, cognition, tokenism or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
4. Links levels: how organizational status, structure, hierarchy, life stage or field-level actors shape or condition individual, team or organizational behavior and outcomes.
5. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning, behavioral theory of the firm, attention, exploration and exploitation, search, categories, entrepreneurial creation, status dynamics.
6. Frames the contribution as resolving a tension between prevailing theory and emerging evidence, or between competing mechanisms, not just estimating an effect.
7. Uses experiments or multimethod designs to isolate actor or audience mechanisms theorized in advance, rather than mainly reporting effect sizes of a practice's consequences.
8. Accepts formal or simulation models of organizational processes, such as learning, search, judgment aggregation, or divergent beliefs among organizational actors, deriving testable propositions.
9. Builds moderators from named theoretical mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, rather than from convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
10. Theorizes interplay between formal controls (monitoring, incentives, contracts) and informal or normative ones (values, trust, relationships), including substitution or crowding out.
11. Explains individual or team outcomes through relationships, networks, social integration or hierarchy position, including how actors' beliefs about hierarchy shape tie formation.
12. Contextualizes familiar individual-level effects (pay gaps, human capital, status) by showing which organizational conditions amplify, erase or reverse them.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Economics-style effect estimation or tests of classic economic theories (demand-side discrimination, real options, network effects) against wages, prices or sales, closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions.
2. Principal-agent or mechanism-design models where a principal optimizes incentives with no role for divergent beliefs, cognition or organizational process.
3. The main contribution is a new measure, dataset, research method, computational technique or descriptive typology rather than a theoretical mechanism about organizations or audiences.
4. Corporate-strategy outcomes (divestiture, diversification, M&A, competitive advantage) tested on firm panels with audience or social mechanisms only inferred; also literature reviews or research agendas.
5. Main explanation is cross-national legal origins or country regulation with legal implications; broad social context like generalized trust is fine when theorized as shaping organizing.
6. Exploratory or null-result pattern-finding whose mechanism is guessed afterward, e.g., results described only as 'suggestive of' a mechanism, with no prior theoretical argument.
7. Explains outcomes through individual skill or human-capital accumulation alone; human capital is fine when conditioned by organizational structure, power or life stage.
8. Framed mainly as advancing individual psychology (effort, motivation, psychological experience) without consequences for relationships, networks, social structure or organizing.
