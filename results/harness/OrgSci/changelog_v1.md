# Changelog v1 (revision of v0)

**ACCEPTED** at iteration 1: val accuracy 88.3% (vs SMJ 100.0%, vs MgmtSci 76.7%); best so far 80.0%, threshold 79.0%. Revision of v0.

Train pool: v0 judged 38 train pairs, accuracy 88.2% (vs SMJ 100.0%, vs MgmtSci 76.3%). Reflection saw 5 misjudged, 4 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **ADDED**: Theorizes how audiences such as investors, analysts, customers or job seekers read signals, claims, categories or actor attributes to form evaluations.
  - *why*: Most Organization Science winners (pitch language, exemplar similarity, board diversity signals, authenticity claims, CEO traits seen by lenders) are about audiences interpreting cues.
- **ADDED**: Posits countervailing effects, paradoxes or trade-offs, such as a benefit at one stage or condition and a penalty at another, and specifies contingencies.
  - *why*: Winners kept arguing two-sided effects: screening versus assessment, penalty versus premium, a self-limiting group advantage, an authenticity paradox.
- **ADDED**: Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, categories, identity, authenticity, cognition, tokenism or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
  - *why*: Organization Science articles use mechanisms from sociology and psychology, while the other journals' articles leaned on economic efficiency logic.
- **ADDED**: Links individual cognition or behavior to organizational systems, structures, governance arrangements or group processes, explaining how context changes what individuals do.
  - *why*: The winner linked individual cognitive states to organizational ethics systems; the loser looked at peer diffusion and hiring policy.
- **ADDED**: Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning, exploration and exploitation, search, optimal distinctiveness, categories, entrepreneurial creation, status dynamics.
  - *why*: Winners spoke directly to the journal's core theory conversations.
- **ADDED**: Frames the contribution as resolving a tension between prevailing theory and emerging evidence, or between competing mechanisms, not just estimating an effect.
  - *why*: The certification, group learning and board-reform winners framed themselves around reconciling tensions or comparing mechanisms.
- **ADDED**: Uses quasi-experiments or field data to tell theoretical mechanisms apart (e.g., symbolic versus substantive), not only to test whether an intervention works.
  - *why*: Both board-reform papers used difference-in-differences; the winner used it to separate representation from empowerment, while the loser only asked whether one law worked.
- **ADDED**: Accepts formal, computational or simulation models that derive propositions about actors' beliefs, misalignment, learning or organizational processes.
  - *why*: The board wrongly rejected a formal model of belief misalignment; Organization Science also publishes simulation work.
- **ADDED**: Builds moderators from named theoretical mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, rather than from convenient proxies or practical boundary conditions.
  - *why*: A tiebreaker for pairs where both sides test hypotheses: winners tied their moderators to named theory.
- **ADDED**: Economics-style evaluation of a program, law or practice using exogenous variation, closing with efficiency or policy prescriptions instead of organizational theory.
  - *why*: Losing papers on mentoring advice, dishonesty diffusion and a hiring-committee law were econ-style intervention studies with policy conclusions.
- **ADDED**: Formal incentive or contracting models centered on leverage, wages or effort efficiency, backed by stylized correlations, suggest economics or finance journals.
  - *why*: The debt and relational-incentives model lost to an upper-echelons study of how stakeholders perceive executives.
- **ADDED**: The main contribution is a new measure, dataset or descriptive typology rather than a theoretical mechanism about organizations or audiences.
  - *why*: Losers put their novel measures or typologies first, ahead of theory.
- **ADDED**: Framed mainly around competitive advantage, industry evolution or strategic investment outcomes for strategy audiences, with thin sociological or behavioral mechanism.
  - *why*: Anchor losers (industry evolution, client transfer after employee mobility) were framed for strategy audiences.
- **ADDED**: The main explanation is cross-national culture, legal origins or country institutions, with legal or regulatory implications, which points to international business or governance outlets.
  - *why*: The cross-country study of directors' values and legal origins lost to an organizational governance model.
- **ADDED**: Openly exploratory pattern-finding whose mechanisms are guessed after the fact, with no theoretical argument set out in advance.
  - *why*: An exploratory board-diversity study, with mechanisms inferred from interviews afterwards, lost to a signaling-theory argument.
- **ADDED**: Explains outcomes through individual skill or human-capital advantages from learning-by-doing, without social, cognitive or organizational process.
  - *why*: The losing mentoring paper explained its results by one group of mentors having a skill advantage, not by any organizational mechanism.
