## What Organization Science values
1. Theorizes how audiences, including lenders and investors, infer unobservable qualities such as intent, risk or competence from claims, language, categories or seemingly unrelated actor attributes.
2. Posits countervailing effects, paradoxes or trade-offs, and specifies contingencies using moderators derived from named mechanisms about actors, audiences or organizations, not convenient proxies.
3. Traces how audience expectations feed back into organizational choices, e.g., claims or identities that constrain a producer's growth, scale, production or repositioning.
4. Explains with sociological or behavioral constructs such as status, legitimacy, categories, identity, authenticity, cognition, tokenism or symbolic compliance, not mainly economic efficiency.
5. Explains individual or team outcomes, including careers and entrepreneurship, through organizational structures: hierarchy, formal arrangements, firm size, internal opportunities, networks or social integration.
6. Advances core organization-theory conversations: organizational learning, exploration and exploitation, search, optimal distinctiveness, categories, entrepreneurial creation, status dynamics.
7. Frames the contribution as resolving a tension between prevailing theory and emerging evidence, or between competing mechanisms, not just estimating an effect.
8. Uses experiments, quasi-experiments, policy-capturing or mixed methods to separate mechanisms or reveal hidden trade-offs of a practice, not only whether it works.
9. Accepts formal or simulation models of organizational decision processes, such as judgment aggregation, learning, search or unintended consequences of practices, deriving propositions rather than optimal incentive designs.
10. Theorizes interplay between formal controls (monitoring, incentives, contracts) and informal or normative ones (values, trust, relationships), including substitution or crowding out.
11. Theorizes how inter-organizational collaborations or shared standards reshape firm boundaries, vertical industry structure, or the capabilities firms need, weighing competing mechanisms.
12. Examines workers' subjective experience of status, identity or fit across work contexts, often grounding hypotheses in qualitative data before testing them.

## What Organization Science does not value / red flags
1. Economics-style effect estimation or policy evaluation ending in efficiency prescriptions; effect sizes alone are fine when organizational mechanisms drive the argument.
2. Principal-agent, mechanism-design, relational-contract or individual sorting-and-self-learning models, where optimizing agents or discounted-value logic drive results, suggest economics or finance journals.
3. Main contribution is a new measure, index, dataset or typology; such papers lose to same-topic papers advancing a substantive organizational mechanism, even effect-estimating ones.
4. Framed for strategy audiences, such as competitive advantage, M&A or capability performance, or industry evolution, with thin behavioral mechanism; also literature reviews or research agendas.
5. Main explanation is cross-national legal origins or country regulation with legal implications; broad social context like generalized trust is fine when theorized as shaping organizing.
6. Openly exploratory pattern-finding whose mechanisms are guessed after the fact, with no theoretical argument set out in advance.
7. Explains career or productivity outcomes through individual skill or human-capital accumulation alone; human capital is fine as a moderator of social or organizational processes.
8. Tests a classic economic theory, such as taste-based discrimination, network effects or foreclosure, against market outcomes like prices, revenues or sales.
