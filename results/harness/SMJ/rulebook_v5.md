## What Strategic Management Journal values
1. The firm, or the founder acting for the venture, is the decision-maker, and the abstract studies a strategic choice such as entry, positioning, investment, scope or innovation direction.
2. The outcomes are at firm level: performance, value creation, growth-option value, revenue or competitive advantage, not only individual or audience outcomes.
3. Effects depend on contingencies: the abstract shows how strategy–environment fit or firm and industry characteristics strengthen or weaken the main relationship.
4. The abstract names the costs, trade-offs or unintended penalties of strategic moves for the firm, such as change, repositioning, path dependence or departing from tradition.
5. Individual-level mechanisms count when they explain strategic actions or firm results; lab, manager or in-firm field experiments that measure firm-level outcomes such as revenue qualify.
6. Governance and leadership: boards, top teams, CEO succession or dismissal, and executive motives such as status rivalry that drive strategic actions and their value consequences.
7. Hypotheses come from core strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral theory, capabilities, competitive dynamics, transaction costs) and are tested with archival, experimental or qualitative evidence.
8. The abstract explicitly claims contributions to strategy literatures, such as competitive, corporate, complementor, entrepreneurial or international strategy, and states what managers should do.
9. Customer or audience reactions are framed as determinants of the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice, not as phenomena of evaluator cognition in their own right.
10. Reviews, conceptual syntheses, or replications and re-examinations of influential firm-level studies that guide strategy researchers fit, even without new theory or data.
11. Network positions, political connections or stakeholder ties are treated as firm-level sources of advantage, resource access, risk or boundary choices, often under environmental shocks.
12. Qualitative studies fit when they show how strategy-making processes (formulation, collaboration, participant selection) produce different strategic outcomes, linked to strategy research.

## What Strategic Management Journal does not value / red flags
1. The main contribution is a formal model deriving optimal policies, equilibria or new mathematical constructs, without empirical testing or a synthesis for strategy research.
2. Economics or finance framing: corporate-finance mechanisms, market-price tests, IO-style effect-size estimates without management theory, or macro and public-policy outcomes.
3. The outcome is a personnel or personal reward (pay, turnover, promotion, executive compensation, gender gaps) with no measured effect on firm performance or strategic action.
4. A sociological focus on categories, identity or evaluator perception, framed mainly within organization theory, where the firm's strategic value is absent.
5. Inductive case studies building process theory for organization-theory debates (meaning-making, change resistance, identity) rather than explaining strategic outcomes suggest another outlet.
6. Conceptual or simulation arguments about organizational adaptation or reconciling paradigms that are not anchored in a core strategy theory.
7. Executive or political behavior explained mainly by individual motives or ideology (donations, self-promotion), rather than as firm strategy affecting firm resources or value.
8. An intervention or program is evaluated only on individual-level outcomes, without theory about strategic choices or firm-level results such as revenue or productivity.
