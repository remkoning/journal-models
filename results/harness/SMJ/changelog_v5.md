# Changelog v5 (revision of v4)

**ACCEPTED** at iteration 5: val accuracy 93.3% (vs OrgSci 93.3%, vs MgmtSci 93.3%); best so far 93.3%, threshold 92.3%. Revision of v4.

Train pool: v4 judged 40 train pairs, accuracy 95.0% (vs OrgSci 90.0%, vs MgmtSci 100.0%). Reflection saw 2 misjudged, 7 low-confidence and 3 anchor pairs.

- **EDITED**: Individual-level mechanisms count when they explain strategic processes (search, competitive response, decision-making) or firm results; lab or manager experiments qualify.
  - *now*: Individual-level mechanisms count when they explain strategic actions or firm results; lab, manager or in-firm field experiments that measure firm-level outcomes such as revenue qualify.
  - *why*: SMJ published in-firm field experiments on employees when they also measured firm revenue; the board was penalizing them.
- **EDITED**: The study covers strategic leadership and governance: top teams, dominant coalitions, family control or CEO succession, and their link to firm strategy or performance feedback.
  - *now*: Governance and leadership: boards, top teams, CEO succession or dismissal, and executive motives such as status rivalry that drive strategic actions and their value consequences.
  - *why*: SMJ articles covered board dismissal decisions and CEO motives that drive acquisitions; the rival articles covered CEO personal rewards.
- **EDITED**: Reviews, essays or conceptual pieces that synthesize, extend or guide use of a strategy theory for strategy researchers fit, even without new data.
  - *now*: Reviews, conceptual syntheses, or replications and re-examinations of influential firm-level studies that guide strategy researchers fit, even without new theory or data.
  - *why*: SMJ published a replication of an influential firm-survival study, and the board ranked it below a study with new theory.
- **EDITED**: Network positions, prior relationships or stakeholder ties are treated as firm-level sources of advantage, risk or boundary choices, often under environmental shocks.
  - *now*: Network positions, political connections or stakeholder ties are treated as firm-level sources of advantage, resource access, risk or boundary choices, often under environmental shocks.
  - *why*: SMJ framed corporate political connections as firm access to public resources, while the rival focused on individual donor motives.
- **EDITED**: Qualitative studies fit when they examine how strategy-makers formulate, justify or communicate strategy and explicitly link findings to strategy research.
  - *now*: Qualitative studies fit when they show how strategy-making processes (formulation, collaboration, participant selection) produce different strategic outcomes, linked to strategy research.
  - *why*: The SMJ qualitative study explained how strategy-process dynamics lead to different outcomes; the rival added to the literature on meaning-making and change.
- **EDITED**: The dependent variable is a personnel outcome such as turnover, exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, even when it is framed as relevant to the firm.
  - *now*: The outcome is a personnel or personal reward (pay, turnover, promotion, executive compensation, gender gaps) with no measured effect on firm performance or strategic action.
  - *why*: The old rule wrongly penalized SMJ papers that measured retention together with firm revenue, and CEO-departure governance papers; it should target personnel or pay outcomes with no firm link.
- **EDITED**: An inductive case study that builds process theory framed in organization theory, with no tie to strategy-making or strategy literatures, suggests another outlet.
  - *now*: Inductive case studies building process theory for organization-theory debates (meaning-making, change resistance, identity) rather than explaining strategic outcomes suggest another outlet.
  - *why*: Both qualitative rivals used the word 'strategic'; the real difference is whether the claimed contribution is to organization-theory debates or to strategic outcomes.
- **DROPPED**: The main contribution is settling a debate between theories of the firm, rather than explaining a strategic choice and what follows from it.
  - *why*: None of these pairs relied on it, and the slot was needed for a more frequent pattern about individual executive motives.
- **ADDED**: Executive or political behavior explained mainly by individual motives or ideology (donations, self-promotion), rather than as firm strategy affecting firm resources or value.
  - *why*: The non-SMJ rivals explained executive donations or media appearances by personal motives; the SMJ papers tied this behavior to firm resources or acquisitions.
- **EDITED**: The abstract mainly describes an intervention or training design and its causal effect, without theory-derived hypotheses about strategic choices or firm outcomes.
  - *now*: An intervention or program is evaluated only on individual-level outcomes, without theory about strategic choices or firm-level results such as revenue or productivity.
  - *why*: SMJ accepts RCTs that report firm-level results, so the flag should apply only when outcomes stay at the individual level.
