# Strategic Management Journal rulebook v1

**ACCEPTED** at iteration 1: val accuracy 93.3% (vs OrgSci 93.3%, vs MgmtSci 93.3%); best so far 91.7%, threshold 90.7%. Revision of v0.

## What Strategic Management Journal values
1. The firm, or the founder acting for the venture, is the decision-maker, and the abstract studies a strategic choice such as entry, positioning, investment, scope or innovation direction.
2. The outcomes are at firm level: performance, value creation, growth-option value, revenue or competitive advantage, not only individual or audience outcomes.
3. Effects depend on contingencies: the abstract shows how strategy–environment fit or firm and industry characteristics strengthen or weaken the main relationship.
4. The abstract names the costs, trade-offs or unintended penalties of strategic moves for the firm, such as change, repositioning, path dependence or departing from tradition.
5. Individual or employee-level mechanisms (micro-foundations) are explicitly tied to firm-level results, such as leaders affecting performance or worker interventions raising revenue.
6. The study covers strategic leadership and governance: top teams, dominant coalitions, family control or CEO succession, and their link to firm strategy or performance feedback.
7. Hypotheses are derived from core strategy theories (resource-based, real options, behavioral theory of the firm, capabilities, competitive dynamics) and tested on archival or field-experimental data.
8. The abstract explicitly claims contributions to strategy literatures, such as competitive, corporate, complementor, entrepreneurial or international strategy, and states what managers should do.
9. Customer or audience reactions are framed as determinants of the value or cost of a firm's strategic choice, not as phenomena of evaluator cognition in their own right.

## What Strategic Management Journal does not value / red flags
1. A formal or analytical model deriving optimal design, pricing or fee policies without empirical testing suggests an operations, economics or marketing journal.
2. An economics or finance framing: testing information asymmetry with market-price predictions, macro or local economic outcomes, or impacts at the level of public policy.
3. Personnel or labor outcomes such as exit, promotion, aspirations or gender gaps, studied without any link to firm strategy or firm performance.
4. A sociological focus on categories, identity or evaluator perception where the producer is an individual and the firm's strategic value is absent.
5. An inductive comparative case study that builds a process theory from a handful of firms suggests a qualitative organization-theory outlet.
6. Purely conceptual or simulation arguments about organizational adaptation, or about reconciling theoretical paradigms, with no empirical test of strategic outcomes.
7. The main contribution is settling a debate between theories of the firm, rather than explaining a strategic choice and what follows from it.
